## Slide 09 · (a) Membrane polarization

Electrolytic conduction, ions moving in pore water, dominates in most rocks at low frequencies without metallic minerals. It needs porosity with interconnected fluid. Most minerals, especially clay, carry a net negative surface charge, so positive ions are attracted and negative ions repelled, as the upper diagram shows. This positive-ion zone extends about $10^{-6}$ cm, or 10 nm, into the fluid. Where a pore narrows to that width under dc voltage, the zone acts as an ion-selective membrane, as the lower diagram shows. Negative ions accumulate at one end and leave the other, so the ion distribution polarizes and the current is impeded. Their return after cut-off is the Induced Polarization (IP) decay. No metal is needed, so every rock shows some IP effect.

## Slide 20 · Chargeability M

Chargeability $M$ is the most common time-domain Induced Polarization (IP) quantity. It integrates the decay curve over a window and divides by the steady voltage:
$$M = \frac{1}{V_c}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} V(t)\,dt$$
We can get an idea from this equation that a larger area under the decay curve, the hatched region between $t_1$ and $t_2$, gives a larger $M$. The volts cancel, leaving milliseconds. The Newmont receiver integrates from 0.45 to 1.1 s in a 2 s on-off pulse. Different windows catch different parts of the decay, so instruments are not directly comparable. With the Newmont window, limestone gives about 10 to 20 ms, shale 50 to 100 ms, and rock with 8 to 20 percent sulfides 1000 to 2000 ms. A high $M$ is the classic pointer to disseminated sulfides.
