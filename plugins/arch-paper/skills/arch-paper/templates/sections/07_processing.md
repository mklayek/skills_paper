# Data Processing — template

## Purpose & writing logic

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Processing section narrates the signal-processing pipeline that turns raw radargrams into time slices, amplitude maps, and depth-converted images. Across the three source papers it is written as a strict chronological chain. Goodman et al. (1995) walk from resampling, through static correction, through amplitude-squared energy averaging, through gridding, and then state plainly that migration and deconvolution were not applied and why. Goodman et al. (2014) give the fullest step list in one sentence, "dewowing, bandpass filtering, Kirchoff migration and Hilbert transform," followed by the slice-generation scheme. Dalan et al. (2011) name the software (GPR-SLICE) and the overlay-analysis step and outsource the deeper math to cited programs.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Generally the writing logic is: name each operation in the order performed, then explain how the slices or amplitude maps were built, then convert time to depth with a stated velocity. In this case the reusable move is to be explicit about what was not done and to flag the interpretive distortion that the simplifying choice introduces, since Goodman et al. (1995) warn that unmigrated data make anomalies look larger and deeper than they are. That's why the section reads as a defended pipeline, not a checklist.

## When to write / what belongs here

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Write this after Data Acquisition and before Results. It belongs here whenever the reader needs to know how the displayed images were produced before trusting an interpretation. In this case the section owns the full chain: dewow, time-zero, gain, filtering, migration, time-slice and amplitude-map creation, and depth conversion. The velocity and depth equations themselves live in `08_equations_math.md`; reference them here rather than re-deriving them.

What belongs here:
- Editing and correction (dewow, time-zero, static or topographic correction).
- Gain and normalization.
- Filtering (bandpass, background removal).
- Migration and, where used, Hilbert transform or envelope.
- Time-slice and amplitude-map creation (window thickness, averaging, gridding).
- Time-to-depth conversion with a stated velocity.
- An explicit statement of any step omitted and its interpretive consequence.

## Recommended paragraph flow

1. State the editing and correction steps first (dewow, time-zero, static or topographic correction).
2. State gain and normalization, and the reason for gaining images rather than raw radargrams if that choice is made.
3. State filtering and migration, naming the migration algorithm.
4. Describe time-slice or amplitude-map creation: window thickness, squared-amplitude averaging, gridding method, pixel size.
5. State the time-to-depth conversion and the velocity used, cross-referencing the equations template.
6. State what was not done (deconvolution, full velocity modeling) and the interpretive distortion it leaves.

## Expected scientific content

- Dewow and time-zero correction.
- Static or topographic correction where relief matters.
- Gain and normalization scheme, with rationale.
- Bandpass filtering and any background removal.
- Migration algorithm named (for example Kirchhoff).
- Hilbert transform or envelope where used.
- Time-slice and amplitude-map creation: window thickness, squared-amplitude averaging, gridding method, pixel size.
- Time-to-depth conversion with a stated velocity and how it was obtained.
- An explicit statement of any omitted step and its interpretive consequence.
- Software name and version.

## Citation strategy

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Generally cite the origin of each named technique and outsource heavy math to a cited program or companion paper. In this case cite the time-slice method to its source (Goodman et al., 1995), cite the processing software and, where forward or inverse modeling is used, cite the external program that performs it, the way Dalan et al. (2011) cite Res2Dinv and Blakely (1995). Use author-year in-text refs `(Author et al., 2023)`, sentence-final, one citation per named step, and avoid citation stacking.

## Figure/table references typical here

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A method-demonstration figure that pairs raw and processed (or migrated and unmigrated) radargrams is typical here. A multi-panel depth-slice progression figure and an isosurface rendering are commonly first introduced in this section or in Results. Refer to figures as "Figure N" spelled out in body text, and let captions carry the processing detail so a reader can reconstruct the pipeline from the figures alone.

## Tense & voice guidance

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Generally write Processing in the simple past, passive voice, since the operations are completed and the data are the subject. So write "Bandpass filtering was applied." However, a general statement of what a step does, or what the current figure shows, takes the present tense. Throughout, avoid first person for routine steps; reserve any active voice for a genuine methodological judgment.

## Common phrases / sentence openers

- "Averages of the squared amplitude of the reflected radar signal over a horizontal spatial window and a vertical time (depth) window were used as the time slice parameter to be mapped."
- "Migration or deconvolution was not applied to any of the data in this study."
- "Radargram signal processes included dewowing, bandpass filtering, Kirchoff migration and Hilbert transform."
- "The net effect of using unmigrated radargrams is that the size of anomalies as well as their apparent time (depth) locations can be distorted."
- "Depth calibration for the site was estimated by matching to a faint hyperbola seen on one of the radargrams."
- "The interpolation of the normalized 2D images into 3D volumes effectively uses the automatic gaining in the images."

## Short worked example

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Raw radargrams were dewowed and time-zero corrected, and topographic corrections were applied over the sloping mound using the surface normal derived from the survey topography. A spreading-and-exponential gain was applied, and each slice was independently normalized so that weak deep reflections remained visible. Signal processing then included a 130 to 400 MHz bandpass filter and Kirchhoff migration, followed by a Hilbert transform to recover the reflection envelope. Time slices were generated by averaging the squared amplitude within 30 cm depth windows spaced 0.25 m along-line, then gridding with an inverse-distance algorithm at 5 cm pixels. Two-way travel time was converted to depth using a velocity of 7 cm/ns estimated by hyperbola fitting, following the relation in the equations section. Deconvolution was not applied, because the broadband pulse is dispersive and the site dielectric structure was not fully known; the net effect is that anomaly sizes and apparent depths are slightly exaggerated.
