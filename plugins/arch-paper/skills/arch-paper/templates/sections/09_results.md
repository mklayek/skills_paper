# Results — template

## 3. Purpose & writing logic

The Results section reports what the geophysical data actually shows, one target at a time, before any deep archaeological argument is built on top of it. Across the three Goodman papers the Results are never a bare dump of numbers. Generally the section walks the reader through each site or anomaly as a short discovery narrative that moves in a fixed order, from acquisition fact to observed pattern to a first-order reading of that pattern. In the 1995 time-slice paper the Results live inside the "Application to..." site sections and step slice by slice from shallow to deep, which mirrors how a practitioner flips through a time-slice stack. In the 2011 Biesterfeldt paper the Results mirror the Methods heading-for-heading, so a reader can flip between how a technique was deployed and what it found using the same subheading text. In the 2014 Kofun paper the Results are fused into the "GPR Survey" narrative and told as detection stories that end on an artifact list or a measured dimension.

So the writing logic is: orient the reader to the figure first, describe the anomaly geometry and size in plain observational language, then hand off to interpretation. The Results section holds the observation. It should resist reaching for the full archaeological conclusion, which belongs in Interpretation, but a light gloss that names the anomaly (moat, chamber, post) is normal and expected here because raw reflection values mean nothing to a reader without it.

## 4. When to write / what belongs here

Write Results after Methods is locked, because every Results paragraph points back to an acquisition or processing choice already stated. What belongs here is the observed geophysical evidence and its measured properties. That's why the section is dominated by figure pointers, anomaly descriptions, dimensions in the same units used earlier for the known site features, and explicit statements of what each method did and did not detect. In this case negative results belong here too. Throughout the Biesterfeldt paper the authors state plainly where a method failed, which is a Results fact, not a Discussion opinion.

What does not belong here is the multi-tier archaeological argument, the cross-technique synthesis, or the broader significance. Generally keep those for Interpretation and Discussion. Where the target venue fuses Results and Interpretation, as the 2011 and 2014 papers do, the immediate one-clause gloss stays but the layered evidentiary reasoning still gets deferred to its own passage.

## 5. Recommended paragraph flow

1. Open the subsection with a direct figure pointer that states exactly what is shown and its key parameters, which are slice count, depth range, frequency, or scale.
2. Describe the anomaly geometry, shape, and dimensions using the same units and frame as the site background, so the numbers are directly checkable.
3. State the depth or two-way time at which the feature appears.
4. Give a light naming gloss for the anomaly, hedged where the reading is not yet confirmed.
5. Report negative or null results plainly where a method failed to detect something expected.
6. Note any orientation or alignment check against a known architectural fact, which is entrance direction or post spacing.
7. Cross-reference a second data view or a second method over the same feature when one exists, and state agreement or disagreement with a magnitude.
8. Repeat the whole block once per site or anomaly, shallow to deep, or in the same order Methods used.

## 7. Expected scientific content

- A direct figure pointer opening each subsection with the figure's key parameters stated
- Anomaly geometry, shape, and at least one quantified dimension per target
- Depth or two-way travel time of each feature
- The same units and comparative frame as the site background section
- Explicit negative or null results where a method missed an expected feature
- Orientation or alignment checks against known architectural facts
- Cross-method or cross-representation agreement stated with a magnitude, not a vague "consistent"
- A light naming gloss per anomaly, with confidence language matched to the evidence

## 8. Citation strategy

Citations run sparse here. In the Goodman papers the Results carry almost no methodological citations. Where they appear, they validate a measured dimension against an independent historic or excavation source, as in "which agrees with the dimensions of House 33 recorded by Libby and Stout (Grinnell, 1918)." So cite for cross-validation of a number, and cite the authors' own prior paper only when a named radar pattern needs its published mechanistic explanation, as with the bow-tie pattern citing (Goodman, 1994). Do not citation-stack for general background inside Results.

## 9. Figure/table references typical here

The Results section is the figure-dense heart of the paper. Expect stacked time-slice or depth-slice panels, composite validation figures pairing radargram plus 3D render plus excavation photo, GIS-draped single depth-slice maps, and paired greyscale method maps. Tables are rare in this genre. The three source papers contain zero tables, folding all survey parameters into prose, though a longer journal build can lift those parameters into a survey-parameters table.

## 10. Tense & voice guidance

Use past tense for what was measured or found, which is "were collected," "were found and corroborated." Use present tense for what a figure or slice currently shows to the reader, which is "the ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter," "Figure 3 shows." The two tenses alternate sentence by sentence and that's correct. Keep the voice passive for procedure and observation, and reserve the collective "we" only for a moment of interpretive judgment, as in "where we would expect a floor reflection." Avoid first person otherwise.

## 11. Common phrases / sentence openers

- "Shown in Figure 1 are time slices of parallel profiles made at the Nutubaru Site."
- "On the deepest slice, 40-48 ns, a large ring-like anomaly is imaged."
- "The ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter."
- "which agrees with the dimensions of House 33 recorded by Libby and Stout (Grinnell, 1918, pp. 362-363)."
- "None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit."
- "The chamber ceilings appear around 2.5 m below the ground surface."

## 12. Quality checklist

- [ ] Every subsection opens with a figure pointer stating the figure's key parameters
- [ ] At least one dimension is quantified per target, in the same units as the site background
- [ ] Each measured dimension is checked against an independent source where one exists
- [ ] Negative and null results are stated plainly, not omitted
- [ ] Depth or two-way time is given for every feature
- [ ] Confidence language matches the evidence, with no "confirmed" wording for unexcavated anomalies
- [ ] Results order mirrors the Methods order exactly
- [ ] Tense alternates correctly between past for actions and present for what figures show

## 13. Short worked example

**Results of GPR depth investigations**

Shown in Figure 4 are shallow time slices over Mound 12 and the surrounding field, made every 8 ns from the ground surface down. On the 32 to 40 ns slice a rectangular anomaly is imaged, approximately 4 by 3 m, which sits at the upper end of the 2 to 4 m hut footprints recorded in the 1961 excavation report. The anomaly appears around 1.8 m below the present surface. The four bright corner reflections form a rectangle whose long axis aligns with the documented eastern entrance. A single profile across the feature, shown in Figure 5, reproduces the same reflectors in cross-section. The 40 to 48 ns slice, below the base of the hut, shows no coherent anomaly, so no deeper structure is indicated at this location.
