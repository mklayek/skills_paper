# Study Area and Site — template

**Purpose & writing logic**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section grounds the reader in what is buried and why it matters culturally, before any new geophysics is described, so that later results can be judged against known ethnographic and architectural expectations. The 2011 paper is the clearest model, since it carries a dedicated section titled after the site. It opens with the site name, a precise relative location given as distance and direction from a known city, the landform setting, and an immediate figure pointer, all in the first sentence. It then gives the cultural and historical identity, the dating, and the provenance of the current site maps, followed by quantified feature dimensions drawn from prior excavation, then architectural detail relevant to what geophysics might later detect, such as the four central posts and the central hearth, and finally the land-use history of ploughing that creates the paper's central methodological problem. That last move matters, because the taphonomic history explains why part of the site is erased at the surface and therefore why the survey is needed.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The key writing logic is that every dimension quoted here becomes a benchmark. When Results later report a twelve-metre ring anomaly, the reader can check it against the house diameter recorded in this section. That's why the architectural detail is chosen for its detectability, not for completeness. The 1995 and 2014 papers show that this content does not have to be a standalone upfront section. In the 1995 paper the site background is folded into the opening paragraph of each application section, and in the 2014 chapter the site scale and loss narrative sit inside the Introduction. So in a compressed format the site description can be distributed, but in a full journal article it belongs in its own section, where it primes the reader with vocabulary, dimensions, and stakes.

**When to write / what belongs here**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Write this section after the Introduction and before Methods in a full journal paper, or fold it into the Introduction and the case vignettes in a short chapter. Here belongs the location and geomorphology, the cultural and historical attribution, the dating, the source and georeferencing of site maps, quantified feature dimensions from prior sources, the architectural detail relevant to geophysical detection, and the land-use or taphonomic history. What does not belong is the geophysical acquisition detail, the survey results, or the general theory of the method, which live in Methods and Results.

**Recommended paragraph flow**

1. Open with the site name, a precise relative location as distance and direction from a known city, the landform setting, and a figure pointer, all in the first sentence.
2. Give the cultural and historical identity, the dating, and the provenance of the current site maps, noting how they were georeferenced.
3. Report quantified feature dimensions from prior excavation or historic sources, so later geophysics has a numeric benchmark.
4. Describe the architectural and construction detail specifically relevant to what the survey might detect, such as post arrangements, hearths, and entryways.
5. State the land-use or taphonomic history that erases part of the site at the surface and thereby creates the paper's methodological problem.

**Expected scientific content**

- The site name and a precise relative location by distance and direction from a known place.
- The geomorphological or landform setting.
- The cultural and historical attribution and the dating.
- The provenance and georeferencing of current site maps.
- Quantified feature dimensions from prior excavation or historic record.
- Architectural and construction detail chosen for its geophysical detectability.
- The land-use or taphonomic history that erases or disturbs part of the site.

**Citation strategy**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This section is citation-dense for factual claims, since dimensions and dating are attributed to prior excavation reports and historic sources, often with page numbers, as in `(Grinnell, 1918, pp. 362-363)`. A historic surveyor or excavator can be the sentence subject with `Following Strong (1940), the ditch measured...`. These cross-validation citations do double duty, because the same historic dimensions that anchor the site description are later used to check the geophysical measurements. Grey literature and internal site reports are cited on equal footing with peer-reviewed sources in this sub-field.

**Figure/table references**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The site location map, typically Figure 1, is cited in the very first sentence. A historic site plan or an overlay of the historic map on modern imagery may also be referenced here. Quantified feature dimensions can be gathered into a small site-parameter table if the venue and volume of data warrant it.

**Tense & voice**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Use simple past for historical facts and prior events, as in "the site was partially excavated," and present tense for the site's still-observable state, as in "the village sits on an elevated bench." Voice is impersonal and largely passive. First person is generally avoided in this descriptive section.

**Common phrases / sentence openers**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Real site-description phrases quoted from the analyses:

- "The Biesterfeldt village, located about 70 km southwest of Fargo, North Dakota, sits on an elevated bench overlooking an abandoned meander scar of the Sheyenne River (Figure 1)."
- "Biesterfeldt is a presumed early historic period village of the Cheyenne people, and its presence has been noted since 1799."
- "Based on excavations by Strong 1940, p. 371, the ditch was approximately 3 m wide and 1.5 m deep."
- "The lodges all had four central wood supports with a central hearth."
- "Starting at the beginning of the 20th century, the southern half of the site was repeatedly ploughed; this obliterated all at-surface traces of houses and the ditch in that area."
- "Located about 50 km from the bustling city of Miyazaki, the Saitobaru tombs are unique in Japan..."

**Quality checklist**

- [ ] The first sentence gives the site name, a distance-and-direction location, the landform, and a figure pointer.
- [ ] Cultural attribution and dating are stated and attributed to a source.
- [ ] The provenance and georeferencing of site maps are noted.
- [ ] At least one quantified feature dimension is given as a benchmark for later Results.
- [ ] Architectural detail is chosen for its geophysical detectability.
- [ ] The land-use or taphonomic history that motivates the survey is stated.
- [ ] Every factual dimension carries a citation.
- [ ] No acquisition parameters or geophysical results appear here.

**Short worked example**

> The Saitobaru cemetery, located about 50 km northwest of Miyazaki city, occupies an elevated ash plateau bounded by gradual valleys and distant mountains (Figure 1). The complex is a Kofun-period funerary landscape, dating from roughly the fourth to the seventh century AD, and its current plans derive from Taisho-era surveys georeferenced against modern satellite imagery. Based on prior excavation, the largest keyhole mounds run several hundred metres in length, and the secondary chambers carved beneath them reach ceiling depths of only one to two metres. The chambers were cut into hard-packed volcanic ash, which supports the overburden above an excavated void and, being highly resistive, later proves favourable for radar penetration. Over the past century, farming has flattened many of the smaller mounds, so that weathering has disguised their existence and erased their surface expression across much of the southern plateau.
