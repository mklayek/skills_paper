# Introduction — template

**Purpose & writing logic**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Introduction does several jobs at once, and the three papers show two distinct routes to the same end. The 1995 paper justifies the technique as a presentation format, then establishes an audience problem, which is that archaeologists without geophysics training need radar output translated into something that reads like an excavation plan, and finally positions the paper against a small named body of prior time-slicing work before stating its own contribution. That is a gap justified by audience need rather than by an unsolved scientific problem, so the motivating move is practical, framed around the customer who required an easy way to assimilate the analysis. The 2011 paper takes a different route, opening on the administrative and funding genesis of the fieldwork, then stating the two-part goal, then contrasting the depth focus against the usual plan-view goal of area surveys, and closing on an explicit roadmap sentence that tells the reader exactly how the paper is organized.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 2014 chapter compresses the whole thing into a narrative funnel, since it carries no abstract and the Introduction must serve as one. It opens seventeen hundred years in the past, describes the funerary custom and the landscape, narrows to the prefecture and the named mounds, states the loss narrative where farming and weathering have destroyed the record, and forward-loads the feature taxonomy that later sections lean on. So the reusable logic is a funnel from wide context down to the specific problem, which combines a technique or audience justification, a maturity-gap or loss-gap statement, a forward-loaded vocabulary of feature types, and an explicit roadmap. Generally prior work is framed cumulatively, as a growing but still immature practice, rather than critically, and the section always ends by telling the reader what the paper will do.

**When to write / what belongs here**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Draft the Introduction early, since it fixes the paper's motivating logic, then revise it once the results are known so the roadmap matches the delivered structure. Here belongs the technique rationale, the audience or funding origin, the cultural and historical context in a narrative register, the loss or maturity gap that justifies the work, the taxonomy of feature classes that recur later, the prior-work positioning, and a closing roadmap. What does not belong is detailed acquisition parameters, results, or a full literature review as a standalone block, since in this genre prior work is cited only where it sets up the present survey's continuity.

**Recommended paragraph flow**

1. Frame the technique or the deep historical context, opening with a time-or-place clause rather than "This paper presents," and cite the regional context figure at first mention of the site or monument distribution.
2. State the motivating gap, either an audience-need gap where end users cannot use raw output, or a loss or maturity gap where knowledge has been destroyed or the technique is not yet standard practice.
3. Position prior work cumulatively, naming a few relevant studies, and forward-load the taxonomy of feature classes that Results will use without re-explanation.
4. Close with an explicit one-to-two sentence roadmap of how the paper is organized and why.

**Expected scientific content**

- The technique rationale or the deep historical and cultural context of the site.
- The motivating gap, whether audience-need, loss of the archaeological record, or immaturity of the technique.
- The scale of the site, such as the number of mounds or the survey area, where relevant.
- A cumulative positioning of prior work, naming a few relevant studies.
- A forward-loaded taxonomy of the feature classes that later sections interpret.
- An explicit roadmap stating how the paper is organized.

**Citation strategy**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Citation density is concentrated in the positioning paragraph and is sparse elsewhere. Prior work is framed additively, as in "Several recent papers... have presented," rather than by flaw-finding. Self-citation to the team's own foundational method papers is used for continuity, so the reader knows the present work extends an existing program. Multi-citation lists sit inside one parenthesis separated by semicolons, in the style of `(Kumar et al., 2023)` or `(Terrasse et al., 2015, 2017; Kumar et al., 2023)`, and a study can be the sentence subject with `Following Ge et al. (2024), ...`. In a short chapter, cite prior work only where it directly sets up the present survey's track record.

**Figure/table references**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The regional or context map, typically Figure 1, is cited in the first paragraph at the first mention of the site or monument distribution. No other figures are usually cited from the Introduction, and no tables appear here.

**Tense & voice**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Use present tense for general or timeless claims about the technique, and past or present-perfect for the literature review, as in "have presented" and "have applied." A narrative historical Introduction mixes past tense for events, such as "began to take hold," with present tense for conditions that still hold, such as "the tombs are unique." Voice is impersonal and mostly passive, and first person is generally avoided even when the register is warm. The site, the mounds, or the landscape can become the grammatical subject to animate the narrative.

**Common phrases / sentence openers**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Real Introduction phrases quoted from the analyses:

- "One of the most useful presentations of ground penetration radar (GPR) data sets collected along closely spaced parallel profiles is to display the data in horizontal maps..."
- "the unique requirements of the customer, usually an archaeologist with no background experience in geophysics but with a great deal of experience in making archaeological site plans from excavation, required an easy way to assimilate the radar analysis"
- "None the less, the use of time slicing has still yet to become a widely practiced technique by the GPR user."
- "In this paper the method for creating time slices and their application to studying several archaeological sites is presented."
- "Around seventeen hundred years ago in the southern regions on the island of Kyushu funerary customs began to take hold."
- "The level of destruction of some mounds is complete and no knowledge of their existence might ever be recovered."

**Quality checklist**

- [ ] The opening sentence uses a fronted time-or-place clause, not "This paper presents."
- [ ] The regional or context figure is cited at first mention of the site.
- [ ] A clear motivating gap is stated, whether audience-need, loss, or maturity.
- [ ] Prior work is framed cumulatively and includes justified self-citation.
- [ ] The feature-type taxonomy is defined here for later reuse.
- [ ] The section closes on an explicit roadmap.
- [ ] No acquisition parameters or results leak into the Introduction.
- [ ] Tense separates timeless claims (present) from history and lit review (past or present-perfect).

**Short worked example**

> Around seventeen hundred years ago on the island of Kyushu, a tradition of mounded burial began to take hold across an elevated plateau bounded by valleys and distant mountains (Figure 1). The largest mounds are counted among the region's national treasures, and over three hundred survive in a single concentration that is unique in Japan. Generally, the shallowest elevation on many mounds has been flattened by farming, so that weathering has disguised their existence and no knowledge of some burials might ever be recovered. Following Goodman and Piro (2013), ground penetrating radar offers a route to recover that lost record without excavation. The mounds hold two feature classes, which are shallow surface graves on the mound tops and secondary subterranean chambers carved into the ash below. In this paper the survey method and its application to the Saitobaru cemetery are presented, and in one case the radar interpretation is compared with a subsequent excavation.
