# Conclusions — template

## 3. Purpose & writing logic

The Conclusions restate the paper's core contribution in general terms, defend it against a real objection, and point forward, all in a compact space. Across the three Goodman papers the Conclusions are short and follow a "defend and extend" pattern rather than a plain "here is what we found" summary. The 1995 paper is the clearest model. It reaffirms that time slices are an effective presentation tool, then squarely addresses a known skepticism that ground-surface reflections mask usable data and rebuts it with the paper's own evidence, then points to a companion paper and a broader technique as future directions, and closes on a single forward-looking sentence about improving the utility of the method for the modern archaeologist. The 2011 paper fuses its conclusions into the Discussion and ends on a generalizable claim about combining depth and area survey. The 2014 paper has no formal conclusions and ends on a single chronological interpretation, so a journal build must add an explicit short conclusions paragraph.

So the writing logic is: generalize beyond the specific sites, take on one genuine objection head-on using evidence already presented, and close with the broader significance. The Conclusions earn independent argumentative weight by rebutting a skepticism, not by summarizing.

## 4. When to write / what belongs here

Write the Conclusions last, after Discussion, since they distill it. What belongs here is the generalized restatement of the contribution, an explicit engagement with one real or anticipated objection, forward pointers to related and future work including the authors' own companion papers and a broader technique, and a single closing sentence naming the broader significance. In a longer journal build the Conclusions may also give a compact tally of confirmed versus probable versus unconfirmed features across the sites, and state the demonstrated reliability of the method such as an excavation-confirmed detection record.

What does not belong here is new data, a new anomaly reading, or a rehash of every result. Generally keep the Conclusions to a few tight paragraphs. Where the venue fuses Discussion and Conclusions, the closing generalization and the objection rebuttal still belong at the very end of that merged section.

## 5. Recommended paragraph flow

1. Reaffirm the core contribution in general terms, generalized beyond the specific sites surveyed.
2. Name and rebut one real methodological objection, using evidence the paper already presented.
3. In a longer build, give a compact confirmed-versus-probable-versus-unconfirmed tally and state the method's demonstrated reliability.
4. Point forward to related and future work, citing the authors' own companion papers and a broader generalizing technique.
5. Close on a single sentence naming the broader significance for the end-user discipline.

## 8. Citation strategy

Citations here are few and purposeful. The Conclusions cite the authors' own companion paper to point at a related unresolved issue and extend rather than repeat content, as the 1995 paper does with its topographic-correction companion. They cite one general or classic geophysics reference for the broader technique named as a future direction, as with horizon slicing citing (Telford et al., 1988). So cite to open a door to future work, not to re-support the results, and keep the density low.

## 9. Figure/table references typical here

The Conclusions rarely introduce figures. Any figure reference is a callback to an earlier one when rebutting the objection, so the reader can see that the evidence for the rebuttal is already on the page. Tables are not typical here, though a longer build's confirmed-versus-probable tally can be rendered as a short summary line rather than a table.

## 10. Tense & voice guidance

Use present tense for the generalized claims, which is "are shown to be an effective means," "may also be a preferred form." Use present tense for the standing rebuttal of the objection. Keep recommendations and future pointers in modal or conditional forms, which is "will help improve," "can include." The voice stays impersonal and passive, with the data product or the method as the grammatical subject, and first person is generally avoided in this genre's conclusions.

## 11. Common phrases / sentence openers

- "Time slices of ground penetration radar data sets are shown to be an effective means of integrating and presenting closely spaced profiles."
- "A common conclusion by many users as well as non-believers of the GPR method is that ground surface reflections mask any useable data."
- "The strong ground surface reflections are usually indistinguishable when comparing the top ground surface reflections on the radargrams."
- "Topographically corrected time slices are usually the only proper way to display these data sets in a meaningful way."
- "A more general approach to time slicing can include horizon slicing (Telford et al., 1988)."
- "These and other methodologies will help improve the utility of GPR data sets for the modern archaeologist."

## 13. Short worked example

**Conclusions**

Depth-corrected time slices of GPR data are shown to be an effective means of mapping subterranean burial chambers across a mounded cemetery, and such slices should be generated whenever a dense mound field is surveyed. A common view among archaeologists is that highly disturbed plough-zone soils overwhelm any usable radar signal in the upper metre. The depth-slice data, however, when subtle amplitude changes are compared horizontally rather than read off single profiles, show that shallow surface burials remain imageable even in ploughed ground. Across the four mound groups surveyed, two chambers were confirmed by excavation, three are strongly supported by matching keyhole morphology, and one anomaly cluster remains an unconfirmed prospective target. Earlier work by the authors also examined vector correction for topographic relief, which is particularly important where mound slopes exceed the penetration depth, and a more general treatment can extend this to arbitrary horizon slicing (Telford et al., 1988). These and related methods will continue to improve the utility of GPR for the modern field archaeologist.
