# Abstract — template

**Purpose & writing logic**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The abstract is a scope promise, not a results dump. Across the three papers the abstract compresses the whole study into a single unbroken paragraph and follows a stable rhythm of method, then site, then contribution. The 1995 paper does this in three telegraphic sentences of roughly fifty-five words, stating the technique and data type, then the application sites at country level, then the paper's twin deliverables of methodology and limitations. Notably it names no numeric results and no site-specific outcomes, which is what makes it a process-paper abstract rather than a results-paper one. The 2011 paper runs longer at around two hundred and ninety words but keeps the same four-beat internal logic of general framing, then method enumeration, then site and management context, then findings with a validation statement and a closing generalization. That paper front-loads the breadth of techniques because the span of methods is itself the contribution.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;So the writing logic is to open with a capability statement framed passively, which establishes the general problem space before naming the specific site. Generally the second move names the site with one descriptive clause about its age, culture, or type, and then enumerates what was compared or produced. The final move states what the reader gains, which is the method plus an honest statement of its interpretive limits, and where possible a note that the results were independently confirmed by excavation. That's why the closing sentence carries a limitations clause or a generalization, which signals scientific honesty and sets up the paper's later defend-and-extend argument. The 2014 chapter has no abstract at all, since short edited-volume chapters let the first Introduction paragraph do that work, but any journal-format adaptation must add one.

**When to write / what belongs here**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Write the abstract last, after the body is settled, so the scope promise matches what the paper delivers. It belongs in every journal submission and most proceedings, and it can be omitted only in a short chapter whose opening paragraph serves the same purpose. Here you state the method, the data geometry, the site type at a naming level, the headline outcome, any validation, and the interpretive limits. You do not put derivations, figure references, or citations in the abstract.

**Recommended paragraph flow**

1. Open with a passive capability or problem statement that names the method, the data type, and the acquisition geometry.
2. Name the site with one descriptive clause, then enumerate the specific techniques or products compared or produced.
3. Give the management, research, or heritage context that makes the work matter beyond a pure demonstration.
4. State the headline findings, naming which technique excelled at which sub-problem, and note any independent validation such as excavation.
5. Close with one honest limitations clause or one generalizable claim that reaches beyond this single site.

**Expected scientific content**

- The method name with acronym defined on first use.
- The acquisition geometry, such as closely spaced profiles or downhole soundings.
- The site type and region at a naming level, with one descriptive clause on age, culture, or type.
- The techniques compared or the data products generated.
- The headline finding, ideally with one quantified result in a full journal abstract.
- A validation statement where excavation or prior maps confirmed the result.
- An explicit limitations clause or a generalizable closing claim.

**Citation strategy**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;No in-text citations appear in the abstract in any of the three papers. All source-crediting is deferred to the Introduction and later sections. Keep the abstract citation-free even when the target journal technically permits references there.

**Figure/table references**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;None. Figures and tables are never cited from the abstract.

**Tense & voice**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The 1995 process-paper abstract runs in the present tense throughout, treating the paper's content as a standing artifact with phrasing such as "are developed" and "are presented." A results-heavy abstract like the 2011 paper mixes simple past for what was done, as in "were compared," with present tense for general capability claims, as in "may be gained." Voice is overwhelmingly passive, and first person is generally avoided. Choose present tense for a scope-promise abstract and simple past for a findings-heavy one, then keep it consistent.

**Common phrases / sentence openers**

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Real abstract phrases quoted from the analyses:

- "Time slices of ground-penetrating radar (GPR) data are developed from closely spaced profiles collected at archaeological sites in Japan and the USA."
- "used to remotely sense archaeological features at several burial mounds and a pit dwelling site"
- "the interpretive limitations of the time slices are presented"
- "Information on depth may be gained through the analysis of geophysical maps, by utilizing soundings, pseudosections, or time-slices, or via the application of downhole measurements."
- "Various methods and techniques were compared at the Biesterfeldt site, a proto-historic earth-lodge village in the Northern Plains, USA."
- "These results were confirmed through excavation."

**Quality checklist**

- [ ] The abstract is a single unbroken paragraph within the venue's word limit.
- [ ] The opening sentence names the method, data type, and acquisition geometry.
- [ ] The site is named with one descriptive clause on age, culture, or type.
- [ ] The techniques compared or products generated are enumerated.
- [ ] A limitations clause or a generalizable closing claim ends the paragraph.
- [ ] Any validation such as excavation is stated where it exists.
- [ ] No citations, figure references, or equations appear.
- [ ] Tense is consistent, present for scope-promise or past for findings-heavy.

**Short worked example**

> Depth slices of ground penetrating radar data are developed from closely spaced parallel profiles collected over Kofun-period burial mounds in Miyazaki, Japan. The slices are used to detect subterranean chamber burials and shallow surface graves across a large mounded cemetery where agrarian development has erased many mounds at the surface. Strong ceiling reflections at roughly 2.5 m located two intact chambers, which subsequent excavation confirmed through the recovery of a skeleton, pottery, and bronze mirrors. The processing workflow for producing the depth slices, together with the interpretive limits of unexcavated anomaly clusters, are presented.
