# Interpretation — template

## 3. Purpose & writing logic

The Interpretation section turns geophysical observations into archaeological readings, and it does so on a leash. Across the three Goodman papers the interpretive move is always the same chain, which runs from data to observation to a named archaeological feature, with the confidence of the claim scaled to how much independent corroboration exists. The 1995 paper repeats an eight-step formula for every anomaly, which is parameters, then visual slice observation, then shape and size quantification, then a hedged archaeological label, then a nested sub-feature reading, then cross-representation validation with a second figure, then mechanistic or citational support, then an explicit caveat about confounds. The 2011 paper fuses interpretation into Results but still cross-checks every dimension against an independent source and gives a physical reason for every correlation. The 2014 paper makes the confidence scaling explicit, ranging from excavation-confirmed chambers with recovered artifacts down to anomaly clusters that are only "indicated" and remain unexcavated.

So the writing logic is honesty about evidentiary standing. The centerpiece of this section, distilled chiefly from Goodman (2014), is a five-tier evidentiary-validation hierarchy. Every archaeological claim the paper makes should be placed on that hierarchy, and the hedging language must match the tier. A confirmed chamber is stated flatly. A probable one is "believed to be." An unconfirmed one only "indicates the presence of." Mixing the language up, claiming confirmation for a tier-four anomaly, is the single failure this section exists to prevent.

## The 5-tier evidentiary-validation hierarchy

Generally, place each interpretation on exactly one tier and write it in that tier's register. The hierarchy runs strongest to weakest.

**Tier 1, excavation-confirmed.** The anomaly was dug and the predicted feature was physically recovered. This is the strongest ground truth, and it is stated as flat corroborated fact with no hedge. In Goodman (2014) the Sakamotonoue chambers sit here, validated by "an intact human skeleton along with pottery, swords, bronze mirrors and golden earrings." Language: "was confirmed by excavation," "were found and corroborated by the previous excavation," "revealed."

**Tier 2, strongly-supported.** No excavation of this exact feature, but the interpretation is backed by an independent line of evidence such as a matching historic map dimension, a documented construction method that predicts the observed pattern, or a published physical mechanism. Language: "agrees with the dimensions recorded by," "is consistent with," "as predicted by the bow-tie mechanism of (Goodman, 1994)."

**Tier 3, probable.** The reading rests on morphological or typological reasoning from the geophysical geometry alone, cross-referenced against regional archaeological typology, with no direct dig on this feature. In Goodman (2014) the Mound 202 chronology sits here, inferred from keyhole-bottom elevation. Language: "is believed to be," "most probably represents," "the geometry suggests a."

**Tier 4, possible.** A spatially coherent anomaly is present and reads plausibly as a feature, but the interpretation is offered tentatively with alternatives left open. Language: "may correspond to," "may be related to," "several interpretations suggest," "perhaps."

**Tier 5, unconfirmed.** An anomaly is flagged as a prospective target with no validation yet, explicitly awaiting excavation. In Goodman (2014) the Figure 3 anomaly cluster sits here. Language: "indicate the presence of," "an area that would repay future excavation," never "confirms" or "proves."

A negative or absent anomaly is its own interpretive move and is not a null result to be discarded. Absence of deep reflections below a mound is read as "only a shallow surface burial is present," which is a real archaeological classification.

## 4. When to write / what belongs here

Write Interpretation after Results is stable, since every interpretive claim rests on an observation already reported. What belongs here is the data-to-observation-to-inference chain, the tier placement of each claim, the mechanistic reasoning for why a pattern means what it is claimed to mean, and the explicit caveats about confounds such as antenna-aperture dependence of a pattern. Where the venue keeps Results and Interpretation separate, the observations stay in Results and only the inference lives here. Where the venue fuses them, this section's discipline still applies inline, so the light gloss in Results does not silently harden into a confirmed claim.

## 5. Recommended paragraph flow

1. Restate the observed anomaly briefly, then quantify its diagnostic property.
2. Give the first-order archaeological reading, hedged to its tier.
3. Add any nested sub-feature reading inside the same anomaly.
4. Bring the supporting evidence, which is a second data view, a historic dimension, a construction-method argument, or a published mechanism.
5. Name the evidentiary tier plainly through the hedging language you choose.
6. State the confounds or dependencies that limit the reading.
7. Interpret meaningful absences as feature classifications, not as failures.
8. Close on the archaeological so-what, which is chronology, typology, or function.

## 7. Expected scientific content

- A restated observation plus its quantified diagnostic property per anomaly
- A first-order archaeological reading hedged to its evidentiary tier
- Nested sub-feature readings where an anomaly contains more than one feature
- Independent supporting evidence, whether a second view, a historic dimension, a construction argument, or a published mechanism
- Explicit tier placement carried by the hedging language
- Confound and dependency statements that bound the reading
- Absences interpreted as feature classifications
- A closing chronological, typological, or functional conclusion

## 8. Citation strategy

Citations here support the mechanism, not the archaeology. The Goodman papers cite a published physical model to justify why a pattern looks the way it does, as with the bow-tie pattern and (Goodman, 1994), or cite the potential-field theory behind a depth estimate, as with (Blakely, 1995). The archaeological readings themselves are supported by data, which is excavation, aerial photographs, and historic maps, rather than by literature. So cite to underwrite the physics of an inference and to cross-validate a dimension against a historic source, and lean on self-citation for the authors' own prior mechanistic work.

## 9. Figure/table references typical here

Interpretation leans on the composite validation figure that pairs a radargram, a 3D isosurface render, and an excavation photograph in one visual argument, since that single figure carries the detection-to-confirmation story that anchors a Tier 1 claim. Cross-representation figures matter here too, showing the same feature as both a planview slice and a vertical profile as mutually reinforcing evidence. Interpreted cross-sections with feature outlines overlaid are also common. Tables are not typical.

## 10. Tense & voice guidance

Use present tense for standing interpretive claims, which is "is believed to be a moat," "the ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter." Use past tense for the survey and excavation actions that supply validation, which is "were found," "revealed an intact skeleton." The formula "X is believed to be Y" is the genre's dominant hedge and stays present tense, decoupled from the past-tense survey narration. Keep the voice impersonal and passive for the readings themselves, and let the site or feature be the grammatical subject.

## 11. Common phrases / sentence openers

- "This radar structure is believed to be a moat from an ancient burial mound that is still intact beneath the ground surface."
- "A bright reflection within the ring anomaly is believed to be a collapsed burial."
- "a cluster of several distinct and strong reflections that indicate the presence of intact subterranean chambers beneath the ground here."
- "suggesting that for this mound only a shallow surface burial of 1-2 meters deep is present."
- "Several interpretations suggest that the rectangular anomalies may be related to a series of Mississippian huts."
- "were found with radar and corroborated by the previous excavation."

## 12. Quality checklist

- [ ] Every archaeological claim is placed on exactly one evidentiary tier
- [ ] The hedging language matches the tier, with no "confirmed" wording for unexcavated features
- [ ] Each first-order reading carries an independent line of support or is openly flagged as unconfirmed
- [ ] Mechanistic reasoning is given for why a pattern means what it is claimed to mean
- [ ] Confounds and dependencies that limit each reading are stated
- [ ] Meaningful absences are interpreted as classifications, not discarded as null
- [ ] Each anomaly closes on an archaeological so-what
- [ ] Present tense is used for standing claims, past tense for validating actions

## 13. Short worked example

**Interpretation**

The rectangular anomaly at 32 to 40 ns, roughly 4 by 3 m, is believed to be a semi-subterranean hut floor. Its four bright corner reflections most probably represent the central roof-support posts, since their spacing matches the 2 to 4 m post arrangement documented in the 1961 excavation report, which places this reading at the strongly-supported tier rather than a bare morphological guess. The long axis aligns with the recorded eastern entrance, which is a further independent check. So the corner reflectors are diagnostic of posts only under the assumption that the roof-support pattern was standardized across this village, a caveat carried from the site typology. Below the hut floor no coherent reflections appear, which indicates no cellar or storage pit was cut beneath it, suggesting a single-phase surface structure. Taken together the footprint and post arrangement place this hut in the later occupation phase of the site.
