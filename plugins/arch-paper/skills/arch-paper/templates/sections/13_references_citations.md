# References & Citations — template

## Purpose & writing logic

    This template governs both halves of the citation system in an archaeological-GPR paper: the in-text citation (the short pointer that appears inside a sentence) and the end-of-paper reference list (the full bibliographic record). The two must agree in every author name, year, and spelling. The governing logic across all three source papers (Goodman et al., 1995; Dalan et al., 2011; Goodman et al., 2014) is the author-year (Harvard-style) system: an in-text pointer of the form (Author, year) or Author (year) that resolves to a single alphabetical, unnumbered reference-list entry keyed on the first author's surname. Goodman (2014) is the one outlier that used bracketed numerals in-text, but even there the list was ordered and author-year in form. Prefer author-year for a journal target; it is the Archaeological Prospection house style and the safest default.

    Citations in this genre are sparse and functional, not decorative. They cluster in the Introduction (literature positioning) and in the Methods and Discussion (physical or algorithmic claims), and they thin out almost to nothing in the Results, where the data itself (an excavation, an aerial photograph, a matched dimension) does the supporting work. Citation density tracks methodological and physical claims, not archaeological or interpretive ones. Self-citation to the author's own prior method papers is expected and legitimate, used to establish continuity rather than to pad the list.

## When to write / what belongs here

    Build the reference list continuously as you draft, not as a final pass. Every time you make a claim that rests on someone else's published work, drop the in-text citation immediately and add the full entry to a running list. What belongs in the list: peer-reviewed journal articles, book chapters, whole books and textbooks, conference proceedings and abstracts, and grey literature (internal technical reports, museum reports, unpublished excavation reports). Dalan et al. (2011) treated internal NPS and university field reports as first-class citable sources alongside journal articles, which is normal for this applied sub-field.

    What does not belong: a citation for a claim the data proves on its own (an excavation confirmation, a directly measured dimension), or a citation stack of seven references to prop up a generic "GPR has been widely applied" sentence. Cite one source per specific technique or claim, placed exactly where the claim is made.

## Fill-in skeleton

In-text forms (pick the form that fits the sentence grammar):

```
{{Parenthetical, single}}      ... buried moat that is still intact ({{Author}}, {{year}}).
{{Parenthetical, multi}}       ... beginning to be practiced ({{A}} and {{B}}, {{year}}; {{C}} et al., {{year}}; {{D}} et al., {{year}}).

{{Page-specific historical}}   ... recorded by {{Surveyor}} ({{Author}}, {{year}}, pp. {{n1}}-{{n2}}).
```

Reference-list entries (alphabetical by first-author surname, unnumbered, hanging indent):

```
{{Journal article}}
{{Author A}}, {{Author B}} and {{Author C}}. ({{year}}). {{Article title in sentence case}}. {{Journal Name in Italics}}, {{vol}}({{issue}}): {{page-start}}-{{page-end}}.

{{Book chapter / edited-volume contribution}}
{{Author A}} and {{Author B}}. ({{year}}). {{Chapter title}}, in {{Volume Title in Italics}}, eds. {{Editor A}}, {{Editor B}} and {{Editor C}}, {{Publisher City}}: {{Publisher}}, {{page-start}}-{{page-end}}.

{{Whole book / textbook}}
{{Author A}}, {{Author B}} and {{Author C}}. ({{year}}). {{Book Title in Italics}}. {{Publisher City}}: {{Publisher}}.

{{Conference proceedings / abstract}}
{{Author A}} and {{Author B}}. ({{year}}). {{Paper title}}. {{Proceedings Volume Title in Italics}}, {{Location}}, {{dates if given}}, {{page-start}}-{{page-end}}.
```

    Inline instruction: keep author-name spelling identical between byline, in-text citation, and list. Goodman et al. (1995) misspelled "Nishimura" as "Nishmura" in one in-text list while spelling it correctly in the byline and reference list. Do not emulate that. Spell every journal name out in full and italicize it; bold the volume only if the target journal does so (Dalan et al., 2011 bolded volumes, Goodman et al., 1995 did not). Pick one convention and hold it across every entry.

## Expected content

    A finished reference section is a single alphabetical list, unnumbered, keyed on first-author surname, spanning the whole span of source types the paper draws on. For a full journal article aim for roughly 25 to 45 entries; for a compressed proceedings chapter, 5 to 15 is normal (Goodman et al., 2014 used 5). Preserve the functional mix seen across the source papers: roughly one fifth general archaeological and historical context, two thirds to three quarters GPR and geophysics methodology (including justified self-citation to the author's own foundational method papers), and the remainder comparative case studies from other sites. In-text, every list entry should be cited at least once, and every in-text citation must resolve to a list entry.

## Style / format rules

- Author-year (Harvard) is the default. In-text: (Author, year) parenthetical, or Author (year) when the author is the grammatical subject.
- Multiple works in one parenthesis are separated by semicolons and ordered chronologically then alphabetically: (A et al., 2015, 2017; B et al., 2023).
- Use "et al." for three or more authors in-text; spell all authors out in the reference list.
- Page numbers appear in-text only for historical or direct-quotation sources (pp. 362-363), not for general method citations.
- Place parenthetical citations at the end of the clause or sentence they support, never as a mid-paragraph dump.
- Reference list is alphabetical by first-author surname, unnumbered, hanging indent. Journal names spelled out and italicized.
- Grey literature (technical reports, museum reports) is formatted like any other entry, not segregated.
- Use US English throughout. No em dashes in citation glosses; recast with a comma or a new sentence. Avoid semicolons in your prose (they are reserved here only for separating works inside a citation parenthesis).

## Common phrases / real examples

- "Several recent papers (Milligan and Atkin, 1992; Goodman and Nishimura, 1993; Goodman et al., 1994a) have presented the results of time slicing at various archaeological sites." (Goodman et al., 1995, cumulative literature framing)
- "Goodman (1994) shows how bow-tie radar patterns can be generated from radar profiles taken over V-shaped trenches." (Goodman et al., 1995, author-as-subject)
- "which agrees with the dimensions of House 33 recorded by Libby and Stout (Grinnell, 1918, pp. 362-363)." (Dalan et al., 2011, page-specific cross-validation citation)
- "Although Dalan and Goodman (2007) have previously used GPR time slice software to process downhole susceptibility data, the 2008 NPS course provided an opportunity to use this software." (Dalan et al., 2011, self-citation for continuity)
- Reference entry, journal: "Dalan RA, Goodman D. 2007. Imaging buried landforms using down-hole susceptibility data and 3D GPR visualization software. Archaeological Prospection 14: 273-280."
- Reference entry, book: "Wood WR. 1971. Biesterfeldt: A Post-Contact Coalescent Site on the Northeastern Plains. Smithsonian Contributions to Anthropology Number 15, Smithsonian Institution Press: Washington, DC."

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Every in-text citation resolves to exactly one reference-list entry, and every list entry is cited at least once in the text.
- [ ] Author-name spelling is identical across byline, in-text citations, and the reference list.
- [ ] Multiple works in one parenthesis are semicolon-separated and ordered chronologically then alphabetically.
- [ ] "et al." is used for three or more authors in-text, with all authors spelled out in the list.
- [ ] The reference list is alphabetical by first-author surname, unnumbered, with journal names spelled out and italicized.
- [ ] Citation density is highest in Introduction and Methods, sparse in Results, with self-citation used only for genuine method continuity.
- [ ] Page numbers appear only for historical or directly quoted sources.
- [ ] US English, no em dashes, no stray semicolons outside citation parentheses.

## Short worked example

    In-text, Introduction: "Horizontal amplitude maps have become a standard product in archaeological prospection (Goodman et al., 1995, 2014; Dalan et al., 2011). Following Goodman et al. (1995), we computed each slice as the averaged squared amplitude within a fixed depth window. Conyers (2013) notes that this window thickness controls the vertical resolution of the resulting map."

    Reference list (alphabetical, unnumbered):
