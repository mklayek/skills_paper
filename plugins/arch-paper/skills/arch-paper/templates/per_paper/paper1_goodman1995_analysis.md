## 3. Section-by-Section Analysis

### 3.1 Abstract

**Purpose and writing logic.** The abstract compresses the entire paper into 3 sentences because *Archaeological Prospection* short papers evidently do not require a structured (IMRaD-labeled) abstract. The logic is deliberately telegraphic: state the technique + data type, state where/what it was applied to, state what the reader will get out of the paper (method + limitations) — essentially a promise of scope rather than a summary of findings. No numeric results, no named site outcomes ("moat", "ring anomaly", etc.) appear in the abstract at all — this is a "process paper" abstract, not a "results paper" abstract.

**Paragraph organization.** Single paragraph, no internal breaks.

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "Time slices of ground-penetrating radar (GPR) data are developed from closely spaced profiles collected at archaeological sites in Japan and the USA."

**Typical content:** technique name + acronym definition on first use, data collection geometry ("closely spaced profiles"), study locations at the country level (not named sites), and a closing sentence that frames the paper's two contributions.

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "developed from closely spaced profiles collected at archaeological sites"
- "used to remotely sense archaeological features at several burial mounds and a pit dwelling site"
- "the interpretive limitations of the time slices are presented"

### 3.2 Introduction

**Purpose and writing logic.** The Introduction does triple duty: (1) it justifies horizontal time-slice mapping as a presentation format (paragraph 1), (2) it establishes the *audience problem* — archaeologists without geophysics training need GPR output translated into something that reads like an excavation plan (paragraph 2) — and (3) it positions the paper within a small, explicitly named body of prior time-slicing literature before stating the paper's specific contribution (paragraph 3, split across the column break). This is a classic "gap justified by audience need, not by an unsolved scientific problem" introduction — the rhetorical move is sociological/practical ("customers ... required an easy way to assimilate the radar analysis") rather than purely technical.

**Paragraph organization and flow.** Three main paragraphs in the "Introduction" heading proper:
1. What time slices are and why they're useful (general technique framing).
2. Why archaeological customers specifically need this (the domain-specific justification — this is the paper's real motivating paragraph).
3. A literature-positioning paragraph naming five prior works/venues, concluding that time slicing is not yet "a widely practiced — and a regular data analysis — technique," followed by a short explicit roadmap paragraph ("In this paper the method for creating time slices and their application... is presented...").

Then an italic sub-heading, *Time slice creation*, begins the methods content within the same "Introduction" umbrella — there is no separate "Methods" section title in this paper.

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "One of the most useful presentations of ground penetration radar (GPR) data sets collected along closely spaced parallel profiles is to display the data in horizontal maps that allow easy visualization of the location, depth, size, and shape of radar anomalies buried in the ground."

Note: the paper uses "ground penetration radar" here (not "ground-penetrating radar" as in the Abstract) — an internal inconsistency worth flagging for future authors as a thing to avoid, not emulate.

**Typical content:** technique rationale, audience/stakeholder framing, prior-work citations, explicit paper roadmap/aims statement.

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "Mapping the energy in the reflected radar returns across a survey grid can help to create useful information that can sometimes mirror the general archaeological site plan result obtained from invasive excavation."
- "the unique requirements of the customer—usually an archaeologist with no background experience in geophysics but with a great deal of experience in making archaeological site plans from excavation—required an easy way to assimilate the radar analysis into a form useful for their own research."
- "None the less, the use of time slicing has still yet to become a widely practiced—and a regular data analysis—technique by the GPR user."
- "In this paper the method for creating time slices and their application to studying several archaeological sites is presented."
- "In one case, the archaeological site plans based on the radar survey are compared with excavation site plans."

### 3.3 *Time slice creation* (Methods sub-section, within Introduction)

**Purpose and writing logic.** This is the paper's true Methods section, written as an unlabeled continuation under an italic run-in heading rather than a numbered "2. Methods". It is organized as a step-by-step processing pipeline narrated in strict chronological order of the actual workflow: acquisition geometry → sampling rate → resampling/static correction → amplitude-squared energy averaging → spatial window averaging → gridding parameters → a caveat about anomaly-size inflation from gridding → an explicit statement that migration/deconvolution was *not* applied → a long justification of that choice (dispersive radar physics, unknown complex permittivity, the "aperture dispersion" problem) → the practical consequence for interpretation (apparent-depth error).

**Paragraph organization and flow.** Six paragraphs:
1. Acquisition geometry (zig-zag profiles, 1 m spacing rationale, marker-every-metre, 40–60 scans/m).
2. Resampling to constant 50 scans/m + static corrections + defining the time-slice parameter (average squared amplitude in a spatial × time window).
3. Justification for why *large* window averaging is appropriate for archaeology specifically (a parenthetical aside contrasts this with multichannel seismic-style processing that needs less averaging).
4. Gridding methodology (search radius 2.2 m, inverse-distance weighting exponent 2) and the anomaly-enlargement caveat, with a suggested mitigation (denser grid spacing).
5. An explicit statement that "Migration or deconvolution was not applied to any of the data in this study," followed immediately by the physical justification (broadband, dispersive radar pulses; unknown complex dielectric permittivity/conductivity of protected sites) — this paragraph spans the column break from p.85 to p.86.
6. Introduction of the *aperture dispersion* concept as an unaddressed problem in the literature, followed by a concluding paragraph on the practical interpretive consequence: unmigrated data distort anomaly size and apparent depth, and the true depth of a structure is generally *less* than the depth suggested by the time slice, because "the radar pulse maximum occurs at a time after the first part of the pulse is detected."

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "Radar profiles were collected in a zig-zag direction across the survey grids at each of the sites."

**Typical content:** acquisition parameters (antenna frequency, spacing, scan density), signal processing steps in the order performed, explicit statement of what was *not* done and why, physical/theoretical justification for methodological choices, and a forward-looking caveat about how the choices bias interpretation.

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "The interval chosen between profiles is normally based on the lateral extent of suspected structures."
- "Because the spatial sampling would vary between metre marks due to varying antenna speeds, the data were later resampled to a constant 50 scans m⁻¹ using software."
- "Averages of the squared amplitude of the reflected radar signal over a horizontal spatial window and a vertical time (depth) window were used as the time slice parameter to be mapped."
- "A problem with gridding is that the size of anomalies will tend to be enlarged during the gridding process."
- "Migration or deconvolution was not applied to any of the data in this study."
- "In part the reason for using raw radargrams, given the current level of processing capabilities, is that the radar waves transmitted from the GPR antenna are in fact not monofrequency signals, but have a relatively broad band of energy transmitted around the central frequency."
- "a factor currently not referred to in the literature may go under the heading of *aperture dispersion*"
- "The net effect of using unmigrated radargrams is that the size of anomalies as well as their apparent time (depth) locations can be distorted."

### 3.4 Application to the Nutubaru Burial Mounds

**Purpose and writing logic.** This is the first of two "worked example" sections that constitute the paper's Results + Interpretation combined (there is no separate Results heading). It follows a strict data → observation → archaeological inference chain, moving from shallowest to deepest time slice — mirroring how a reader/practitioner would actually flip through a time-slice stack. Site background (what a "moat," a "Kofun-period mound," farmer disturbance) is given *before* any radar data are shown, so a non-specialist reader has the archaeological vocabulary in hand before interpreting the images.

**Paragraph organization and flow.** Four paragraphs:
1. Site background/archaeological context: what the moats are, when they were built (Kofun period, AD 300–700), why they're invisible at the surface (mound tops removed by farming), how they get accidentally discovered (tractors).
2. Acquisition parameters for this specific survey (300 MHz antenna, 45 cm aperture, 8 ns slice interval, 0–48 ns range) plus interpretation of the shallowest slice (0–8 ns): banded anomalies = current row-cropping direction; brightness asymmetry = soil moisture; a 90° change of direction visible at 16–24 ns tied to a documented change in historical field-cropping orientation (cross-referenced against 1940s aerial photographs) — this is the paper's clearest "corroborating independent evidence" move.
3. Deepest slice interpretation (40–48 ns): the 22 m ring anomaly = an intact buried moat; a bright reflection inside the ring = a collapsed burial chamber; cross-reference to Figure 2 (single profile through the ring) and to the "bow-tie" radar pattern signature, citing Goodman (1994) for the physical explanation (multiples within/between V-shaped trench walls) and noting the pattern's dependence on antenna aperture size.
4. A second, separate anomaly class on the same deepest slice: parallel linear anomalies interpreted as Edo-period (AD 1500–1860) corral walls, with an explicit construction-method argument (soil removed from two parallel trenches, piled between them) used to explain *why* the radar pattern should look the way it does (two parallel bright lines from trench-bottom reflections).

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "An application of time-slice mapping was made at the Nutubaru Burial Mounds in Kyushu, Japan."

**Typical content:** site name/location/period, cultural-historical context, GPR acquisition parameters specific to the site, slice-by-slice description from shallow to deep, named anomaly interpretation with explicit reasoning, cross-reference to independent corroborating evidence (aerial photos, known construction methods), citation of the authors' own prior methodological work to explain a pattern.

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "In many instances there is no surface evidence at Nutubaru that a burial may have existed at the site because the top part of the burial mounds has been removed by ancient and/or modern farmers."
- "These shallow soil structures are clearly visible on the time slices—and undetectable when visually comparing the individual radar profiles."
- "On the deepest slice, 40–48 ns, a large ring-like anomaly is imaged. The ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter. This radar structure is believed to be a moat from an ancient burial mound that is still intact beneath the ground surface."
- "A bright reflection within the ring anomaly is believed to be a collapsed burial."
- "The radar pattern from the moats shows a 'bow-tie' shaped reflection pattern."
- "The brightness of these anomalies are related to reflections from the bottom of the corral (wall) trenches."

### 3.5 Application to the Matsuzaki pit dwelling site

**Purpose and writing logic.** The second worked example, structurally parallel to 3.4 but with an important rhetorical upgrade: this site had *already been partly excavated* (two 1983 test trenches), so the paper can explicitly frame the survey as a **ground-truth validation exercise**, not just a discovery exercise — "The purpose of using radar was to test if the GPR method could accurately image previously known structures for ground truth comparison, in addition to discovering other archaeological structures that may exist at the site." This section therefore has the paper's strongest validation claim (coordinates of two pit dwellings matched between radar and excavation) and explicitly celebrates a second round of independent verification (a 1993 excavation that confirmed additional radar-predicted features).

**Paragraph organization and flow.** Two paragraphs (continuing onto p.88):
1. Site background: two Jomon-period (2000 BC) pit dwellings found in 1983 test trenches, reburied; explicit statement of survey rationale as a ground-truth test; note that a comprehensive excavation was planned immediately after the GPR survey (a real prospective ground-truth design, not just retrospective).
2. Acquisition + slice-by-slice interpretation from 0–8 ns down to 40–48 ns (300 MHz antenna, 80 ns time window): shallow slice shows soil-type asymmetry; 24–32 ns and 32–40 ns show the reburied excavation trenches as linear anomalies, still detectable radar-wise more than 10 years after reburial; specific pit-dwelling coordinates given in-text as (x, y) pairs and matched against excavation; explicit mention that additional, previously undiscovered features (garbage pit, kiln, another pit dwelling) were found by radar *and later confirmed* by 1993 excavation; deepest slice (40–48 ns) below the bottom of the test trenches, showing only the true archaeological features (no trench signal).

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "Another successful application of time slicing was made at the Matsuzaki site in Chiba."

Note the paper telegraphs its verdict in the topic sentence itself ("successful application") — a stylistic pattern worth noting for future authors: Goodman et al. front-load the interpretive conclusion before presenting the evidence, rather than building suspense.

**Typical content:** prior excavation history, explicit ground-truth study design statement, acquisition parameters, slice-by-slice narrative, specific coordinate call-outs matched against known ground truth, explicit forward reference to a later independent excavation as validation.

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "The purpose of using radar was to test if the GPR method could accurately image previously known structures for ground truth comparison, in addition to discovering other archaeological structures that may exist at the site."
- "Even though over 10 years had passed between the trench reburial and the radar survey, the soil disturbances within the trench are still 'freshly' discovered with radar."
- "One pit dwelling within the southern trench at (x = 1 m, y = 7 m) and in the northern trench near (x = 8 m, y = 33 m) were found with radar and corroborated by the previous excavation."

### 3.6 Topographically corrected time slices

**Purpose and writing logic.** This section functions as a methodological extension/discussion: it introduces a third site (Spiro House Mound Site No. 6, Oklahoma) specifically to demonstrate a processing refinement (topographic correction using a nominal velocity) rather than to report a new archaeological discovery per se — although archaeological interpretation (Mississippian huts) is still offered. The rhetorical structure is: state the site + its archaeological significance → explain the correction method and its stated limitation (nominal velocity assumption, since true velocity structure "could not be known completely") → justify why even an imperfect correction is better than none → describe the slice-by-slice topographic/structural behavior as the mound is "cut" progressively deeper → offer an interpretive hypothesis for the deep rectangular anomalies → close with a forward-looking statement that static corrections will become "necessary and regular."

**Paragraph organization and flow.** Four paragraphs:
1. Site background and archaeological significance ("famous for being the western most extent of Mississippian Period mound building in North America"), acquisition parameters (7 ns slice interval), and the topographic correction method with its explicit caveat (nominal velocity of 5 cm ns⁻¹, "some inherent errors will occur by this assumption").
2. Explanation of how anomaly extent at increasing depth mirrors topographic contour position as the "mound is cut deeper," plus an explanation of why shallow slices inside the mound show relatively low reflection amplitude (strong surface-wave interference).
3. Deep-slice interpretation: emerging rectangular reflection patterns hypothesized as sequential Mississippian huts, abandoned/burnt and rebuilt in place.
4. Closing methodological verdict: topographic correction "helped to image recognizable features that were obscurred [sic] in the uncorrected data set," and static corrections are flagged as a future necessity.

**Opening sentence pattern (verbatim):**
> "One example of applying topographic corrections to a parallel profile data set was done for the Spiro House Mound Site number 6, Spiro, Oklahoma."

**Typical content:** justification for a processing refinement, explicit statement of assumptions/limitations of that refinement, site archaeological/chronological significance, structural description tied to topography, tentative/hedged interpretation ("Several interpretations suggest...", "may be related to").

**Characteristic verbatim phrases:**
- "As the velocity structure of the site could not be known completely (as is the case for zero-offset/single channel radar data), a nominal velocity of 5 cm ns⁻¹ was used first to correct the radargrams for topography over the profiles."
- "the completely uncorrected time slices provide a significantly less desirable data set for interpretative purposes."
- "As the mound is cut deeper, the horizontal extent of the anomalies within the survey grid will also indicate the topographic contours existing at that depth."
- "Several interpretations suggest that the rectangular anomalies may be related to a series of Mississippian huts sequentially abandoned or burnt and then reconstructed on or near the remains of the previous structures."
- "Static corrections will become a necessary and regular data process if the full potential of radar time slicing is to be obtained."

## 4. Writing Style Analysis

**Tense usage.**
- **Abstract:** present tense throughout ("are developed," "are used," "are presented") — describes the paper's content as a standing/timeless artifact.
- **Introduction (general framing):** present tense for general/timeless claims about the technique ("is to display," "can help to create"); shifts to present perfect / past for literature review ("have presented the results," "indicate that... are beginning to be practiced").
- **Methods (*Time slice creation*):** past tense throughout, describing what was actually done in this study ("were collected," "was chosen," "were later resampled," "were used"). This is a clean, consistent past-tense narration of a completed procedure — no present tense leaks into methods except for physical/general statements ("the radar waves transmitted from the GPR antenna are in fact not monofrequency signals" — present tense for a general physical fact embedded inside past-tense methods narration).
- **Results/Interpretation (Nutubaru, Matsuzaki, Spiro sections):** mixed — past tense for what was done/found ("Shown in Figure 1 are time slices..."; "were found with radar and corroborated by..."), present tense for describing what the figure/slice currently shows or for standing interpretive claims ("The ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter"; "is believed to be a moat"). The formula "X is believed to be Y" recurs as the paper's dominant interpretive hedge and is always present tense, decoupled from the past-tense narration of the survey itself.
- **Conclusions:** present tense ("are shown to be," "may also be a preferred form").

**Voice.** Overwhelmingly passive voice, especially in Methods and Results/Interpretation: "Radar profiles were collected," "Static corrections... were applied," "Migration or deconvolution was not applied," "is believed to be a moat," "are shown to be an effective means." Active voice is rare and mostly reserved for (a) citing other authors' actions ("Goodman (1994) shows how...") and (b) a few first-person-adjacent framing statements that still avoid "we" (the paper never uses "we" — it is written in a fully impersonal passive register typical of 1990s geophysics journals; agency is either omitted or displaced onto "the paper," "the study," or a cited author).

**Sentence length/variety.** Sentences are long and syntactically dense, frequently 30–50+ words, laden with parenthetical qualifications (e.g., "(as is the case for zero-offset/single channel radar data)", "(at the expense of resolution)", "(as often is the case in the seismic data processes...)"). Short sentences are used sparingly and almost always for emphasis or verdict statements, e.g. "Migration or deconvolution was not applied to any of the data in this study." / "The mathematics for *aperture dispersion* has still to be addressed." Em-dashes are used heavily for appositive/parenthetical insertion rather than commas or separate sentences (e.g., "the unique requirements of the customer—usually an archaeologist with no background experience in geophysics...—required an easy way...").

**Common connector/transition phrases:**
- "In this paper..." (roadmap/purpose statements)
- "Also, to create useful time slices..." (additive transition between method steps)
- "In part the reason for..." (causal explanation opener)
- "In addition, a factor currently not referred to in the literature..." (introducing a new consideration)
- "As these problems... become better understood they will help to..." (forward-looking hedge)
- "None the less,..." (concessive transition — note archaic two-word spelling "None the less")
- "In any event,..." (summarizing/pivoting transition before a section's closing verdict)
- "Shown in Figure N are..." (fronted/inverted construction used repeatedly to introduce each site's results — a recognizable Goodman-paper tic)
- "One example of..." / "Another successful application of..." (section-opening topic-sentence pattern across the three site sections)

## 5. Terminology & GPR-Specific Vocabulary

The paper is notably light on formal glossary-style definitions — nearly all key terms are introduced through **use-in-context** rather than an explicit "X is defined as Y" sentence. This is itself a stylistic signature: Goodman et al. assume a technically literate-enough reader (geophysicists, or archaeologists already somewhat familiar with GPR) and do not pause to define primary terms.

| Term (as it appears in paper) | First-use context / how it is "defined" |
|---|---|
| **time slices / time slicing** | Never given a formal one-sentence definition; introduced operationally through the whole first paragraph of the Introduction — "display the data in horizontal maps that allow easy visualization of the location, depth, size, and shape of radar anomalies... The maps can be created at various time levels within a data set to show radar structures at a specified time (depth) across a surveyed site." This paragraph functions as the de facto definition, spread across multiple sentences rather than one crisp sentence. |
| **ground-penetrating radar (GPR)** | Defined by acronym expansion at first use in the Abstract: "ground-penetrating radar (GPR)"; note the Introduction instead uses "ground penetration radar (GPR)" — an inconsistent expansion of the same acronym within the same paper. |
| **radar anomalies / anomaly** | Not formally defined; used from the first paragraph onward as self-evident ("radar anomalies buried in the ground"). Its meaning is established implicitly through repeated example (moat anomaly, ring anomaly, linear anomaly, rectangular anomaly). |
| **average squared amplitude** (the time-slice parameter) | Explicitly named and operationally specified (not abstractly defined) in Methods: "Averages of the squared amplitude of the reflected radar signal over a horizontal spatial window and a vertical time (depth) window were used as the time slice parameter to be mapped." This is the closest thing to a formal technical definition in the paper. |
| **static corrections** | Named but not defined ("Static corrections and other raw data processes such as unreversing (reversed) profiles, were applied") — assumed familiar to the reader from seismic-processing background. |
| **gridding / search radius / inverse distance weighting exponent** | Introduced operationally with specific parameter values ("most data were gridded with a search radius of 2.2 m and an inverse distance weighting exponent of 2") rather than conceptually defined. |
| **migration** | Not defined; discussed entirely in terms of what was *not* done and why, plus its physical rationale (correcting for point-source diffraction / hyperbolic energy spreading), inferred from context ("The migrating hyperbola (band) would also change with depth, approaching a single hyperbolic line at infinite depth"). |
| **deconvolution** | Same treatment as migration — named as something not applied, discussed via citation to Turner (1994), not defined in-text. |
| **dispersive (radar waves)** | Introduced with a citation rather than a definition: "the radar waves are normally dispersive (Turner, 1994)" — meaning (frequency-dependent velocity/broadening) is implied but never spelled out explicitly. |
| **aperture dispersion** (coined/flagged term) | This is the one term the paper explicitly signals as novel/under-addressed: "a factor currently not referred to in the literature may go under the heading of *aperture dispersion*." It is then explained descriptively over several sentences (antenna aperture size causes near-field broadening of reflections; effect decreases with depth) rather than given a single-sentence formal definition — and the paper explicitly states its mathematics is unsolved: "The mathematics for *aperture dispersion* has still to be addressed." |
| **bow-tie (radar pattern)** | Introduced by visual/pattern description plus citation to the authors' own prior work: "The radar pattern from the moats shows a 'bow-tie' shaped reflection pattern. Goodman (1994) shows how bow-tie radar patterns can be generated from radar profiles taken over V-shaped trenches." |
| **ground truth / ground truth comparison** | Used without formal definition, assumed self-explanatory from archaeological/geophysical practice: "to test if the GPR method could accurately image previously known structures for ground truth comparison." |
| **horizon slicing** | Introduced only in the Conclusions as a named alternative/generalization, with a citation, and a one-clause gloss: "a more general approach to time slicing can include horizon slicing (Telford et al., 1988) where any three-dimensional surface is sliced across a radar data set." This is the paper's second-clearest attempt at a definitional sentence. |
| **radargram(s)** | Used repeatedly (e.g., "raw radargrams," "unmigrated radargrams") without ever being defined — assumed to mean the individual 2D profile record (time/depth vs. position along a single transect), inferred entirely from context and contrast with "time slice" (horizontal) vs. "radargram" (vertical profile). |
| **two-way travel time** | Not used as an explicit phrase anywhere in the paper — the paper works entirely in raw nanosecond (ns) time windows for slice boundaries (e.g., "0–8 ns," "40–48 ns") rather than converting to two-way travel time terminology or explicit "depth" in metres, except via the separate topographic-correction velocity discussion. |
| **dielectric permitivity / conductivity** [sic] | Named together as the source of dispersive velocity information, spelled "permitivity" (missing second "t") in the original text: "the complete velocity information, calculated from the dispersive—complex dielectric permitivity and conductivity from the real ground materials, is also rarely available." No formal definition given. |
| **nominal velocity** | Given a specific operational value without derivation: "a nominal velocity of 5 cm ns⁻¹ was used first to correct the radargrams for topography." |

## 6. Figure Integration

**In-text reference style:** The paper uses the format "Figure N" (spelled out, capital F, no abbreviation "Fig." in body text) when first introducing a figure, e.g. "Shown in Figure 1 are time slices of parallel profiles made at the Nutubaru Site." / "is shown in Figure 2." / "Shown in Figure 3 are time slices made in consecutive 8 ns time windows..." / "Topographically corrected time slices across the site were made every 7 ns from the ground surface down (Figure 4)." Note the figure captions themselves are labeled with the abbreviated form "Fig 1" / "Fig 2" / "Fig 3" / "Fig 4" (no period after "Fig", small label in the corner of the figure page) while the body text always spells out "Figure" in full — a body-text-vs-caption-label style split worth flagging explicitly for future authors.

**Reference pattern:** Always a fronted/inverted "Shown in Figure N are/is..." construction for first introduction of a figure (used 3 of 4 times), with one exception using a trailing parenthetical "(Figure 4)" instead. All four figures are referenced exactly once each in the body text (no figure is referenced multiple times from different sections).

**Caption style/format:** Captions are numbered "Figure N." (full word + period after the number), followed by 1–3 sentences of descriptive/interpretive prose (not just a label) placed directly beneath the image. Captions do not merely describe what is shown but also state the paper's interpretive conclusion about the figure — i.e., captions are argumentative, not neutral.

**Verbatim captions (all four, in full):**

> **Figure 1.** "Time slices calculated for the Nutubaru burial mound site. The lowest time slice shows a large ring anomaly, which is a buried moat (22 m diameter) that is still left intact beneath farmland."

> **Figure 2.** "A radar profile taken across the centre of the ring anomaly imaged at the Nutubaru site. Both of the moat radar anomalies are clearly seen on the profile. A (collapsed) burial chamber is also imaged within the moats."

> **Figure 3.** "Time slices made across the Matsuzaki Jomon period site. A comparison with the excavations—past and present—shows a good correspondence between actual archaeological excavation site plans and the GPR time slices. A photograph of one of the excavated pit dwellings shows them to be characterized by slightly darker soils."

> **Figure 4.** "Topographically corrected time slices of the Spiro House Mound Site number 6, Spiro, Oklahoma. The topographic corrections to the radar profiles were calculated using a nominal microwave velocity of 5cm ns⁻¹ for the entire site."

**Figure types present:**
- **Figure 1** — a stacked "fence diagram" style series of six horizontal time-slice pixel maps (0–8, 8–16, 16–24, 24–32, 32–40, 40–48 ns) for the Nutubaru site, shown in perspective/3D stacked layers with a shared rainbow color scale (dark blue = low, red = high amplitude), axis in metres (0–100 m), full page, no accompanying photo.
- **Figure 2** — a single 2D radargram (vertical profile / time vs. distance section) along "Line 74m," rotated to landscape orientation on the page, with hand-labeled annotations directly on the image pointing to "burial moat," "collapsed burial chamber," "burial moat" (i.e., annotated interpretive labels overlaid on raw data, not a separate legend).
- **Figure 3** — a composite figure: six small time-slice panels (0–6 through 30–36 ns) for the Matsuzaki site arranged in a 3×2 grid with a shared color scale, PLUS a separate hand-drawn schematic site plan (labeled "kiln floor," "pit dwelling," "reburied excavation trench," "garbage pit," "pit dwellings") PLUS a black-and-white field photograph of an excavated pit dwelling with archaeologists working, captioned in-image "excavated pit dwelling." This is the paper's most composite, multi-panel figure (color time-slices + line-art schematic + halftone photo, three distinct graphic media on one page).
- **Figure 4** — six topographically-corrected time-slice panels (0–7 through 35–42 ns) for the Spiro Mound site in a 3×2 grid with shared color scale, axis in metres (0–30 m), no accompanying photo or schematic.

No radargram other than Figure 2 appears in the paper; the dominant figure type is the color-coded planview time-slice map, consistent with the paper's whole thesis (time slices as the primary novel visualization).

## 7. Table Formatting and Captions

**No tables are present in this paper.** All quantitative/parametric information (antenna frequency, scan density, gridding search radius, IDW exponent, nominal velocity, slice time-windows) is conveyed entirely in running prose within the Methods and site-application sections, never in tabular form. This is itself a notable structural feature for future authors to be aware of: a 5-page short paper of this era and venue can be entirely table-free, relying on figures + prose for all data presentation.

## 8. Equations/Mathematical Presentation

**No numbered (or unnumbered) formal equations appear anywhere in the paper.** There is no equation environment, no displayed formula, and no algebraic notation at all. All quantitative relationships are stated in prose with specific numeric values rather than symbolic formulas, e.g.:

- "a nominal velocity of 5 cm ns⁻¹ was used first to correct the radargrams for topography" (velocity given as a bare value, no depth = velocity × time/2 formula written out, even though this relationship is implicitly what "topographic correction" and "time slice depth" depend on).
- "most data were gridded with a search radius of 2.2 m and an inverse distance weighting exponent of 2" (IDW gridding is named and parameterized but the IDW weighting formula itself is not written out).
- Units are given with superscript notation in running text: "50 scans m⁻¹", "5 cm ns⁻¹" — using proper unit exponent notation (m⁻¹, ns⁻¹) but never assembled into a stated equation relating velocity, time, and depth.

**Implication for future authors:** this paper demonstrates that a GPR archaeology paper can be entirely equation-free even when discussing velocity-depth conversion and dielectric permittivity/conductivity concepts — those are handled qualitatively/parametrically, with the underlying physics relationships left implicit or deferred to cited references (Turner, 1994, for dispersive propagation).

## 9. Citation Style

**In-text format:** Author–year, unbracketed, integrated directly into sentence syntax (not enclosed in parentheses when used as the sentence subject, e.g. "Goodman (1994) shows how bow-tie radar patterns can be generated..."), or given parenthetically after a claim when the citation is supporting rather than the subject, e.g. "(Milligan and Atkin, 1992; Goodman and Nishmura, 1993; Goodman et al., 1994a)" and "(Turner, 1994)". Multi-citation lists inside one parenthesis are semicolon-separated. Note the paper misspells "Nishimura" as "Nishmura" in one in-text citation list even though the reference list and byline both spell it "Nishimura" — an internal inconsistency worth flagging.

**Density:** Citations are sparse and clustered — concentrated almost entirely in the Introduction (literature-positioning paragraph) and in specific interpretive claims within the Methods (dispersion physics) and Results sections (bow-tie pattern explanation) and the Conclusions (topographic correction, horizon slicing). The three "Application to..." result sections contain essentially zero citations except where explaining a specific radar pattern (bow-tie → Goodman, 1994) — i.e., citation density tracks *methodological/physical claims*, not *archaeological/interpretive claims*, which are instead supported by the data itself (aerial photos, excavation corroboration) rather than literature.

**Placement patterns:** Citations most often appear as sentence-final parentheticals supporting a general claim ("...indicate that radar time slicing and three-dimensional image rendering of GPR data sets are beginning to be practiced. (Milligan and Atkin, 1992; Goodman and Nishmura, 1993; Goodman et al., 1994a)" — note actually here the citation precedes the "None the less" sentence, appearing as a list before the general claim: "Several recent papers (Milligan and Atkin, 1992; Goodman and Nishmura, 1993; Goodman et al., 1994a) have presented the results of time slicing..." — i.e., citation-list embedded mid-sentence, immediately after "Several recent papers," functioning grammatically as the subject's modifier). A second pattern: citation as sentence subject when crediting a specific finding to a specific paper ("Goodman (1994) shows how bow-tie radar patterns can be generated from radar profiles taken over V-shaped trenches.").

**How prior work is framed/discussed (verbatim examples):**
> "Several recent papers (Milligan and Atkin, 1992; Goodman and Nishmura, 1993; Goodman et al., 1994a) have presented the results of time slicing at various archaeological sites, and some recent conference papers (e.g. Nishimura and Kamei, 1990; Annan et al., 1992; Olhoeft, 1994; Pertinnelli et al., 1994) indicate that radar time slicing and three-dimensional image rendering of GPR data sets are beginning to be practiced. None the less, the use of time slicing has still yet to become a widely practiced—and a regular data analysis—technique by the GPR user."

This is the paper's clearest example of framing prior work: it is treated additively/cumulatively (a growing but still-immature body of practice), not critically or adversarially — there is no explicit critique of any single prior study's methodology anywhere in the paper. The rhetorical move is "others have started to do this, but it isn't yet standard practice — here's a fuller treatment," i.e. positioning by *maturity gap* rather than by *flaw-finding*.

> "Goodman et al. (1994b) also looked at topographically corrected time slices. This is particularly important in the GPR method because topography over a site is sometimes greater than the penetration depths of the survey."

This second example cites the authors' own related prior work (self-citation) to extend rather than repeat content, and immediately follows with an independent justification for why the cited technique matters — prior work is used as a pointer to *related, complementary* material rather than as competition.

## 10. Interpretation Methodology: Data → Observation → Archaeological Inference

The paper's dominant reasoning chain, worked in full for the **Nutubaru ring anomaly / collapsed burial chamber**, proceeds through these explicit stages (traceable directly in the text of Section 3.4 above):

1. **Raw acquisition fact:** "A 300 MHz radar antenna with a 45 cm aperture was used in the survey. Time slices were generated every 8 ns beginning from the top (ground surface) of the data set."
2. **Slice-by-slice visual observation, deepest slice:** "On the deepest slice, 40–48 ns, a large ring-like anomaly is imaged."
3. **Quantification of the observed pattern:** "The ring anomaly is approximately 22 m in diameter."
4. **First-order archaeological inference (hedged, "believed to be"):** "This radar structure is believed to be a moat from an ancient burial mound that is still intact beneath the ground surface."
5. **Secondary, nested inference from a sub-feature within the same anomaly:** "A bright reflection within the ring anomaly is believed to be a collapsed burial."
6. **Cross-validation against a second, independent data view** (a vertical profile rather than the horizontal slice): "One radar profile taken across the burial moats and the collapsed burial chamber is shown in Figure 2." — the paper deliberately shows the *same* feature in two different representations (planview time-slice ring vs. vertical-profile bow-tie reflections) as mutually reinforcing evidence.
7. **Pattern-recognition naming + physical-mechanism citation:** "The radar pattern from the moats shows a 'bow-tie' shaped reflection pattern. Goodman (1994) shows how bow-tie radar patterns can be generated from radar profiles taken over V-shaped trenches." — the inference is strengthened by invoking a previously-published, physics-based generative mechanism (multiples within/between trench walls) that predicts exactly the observed pattern shape, rather than relying on visual analogy alone.
8. **Explicit acknowledgement of a confound/dependency:** "The patterns are also dependent upon the size of the antenna aperture that the signals are transmitted and recorded over" — the paper flags that the diagnostic pattern is not purely a property of the buried feature but also of survey equipment, which is an important epistemic caveat about the limits of pattern-based inference.

This eight-step chain — parameters → visual slice observation → shape/size quantification → hedged archaeological label → nested sub-feature inference → cross-representation validation (second figure/second data view) → mechanistic/citational support for the pattern → explicit caveat about confounds — is the paper's general template for *every* anomaly interpretation in the paper (the same skeleton recurs, compressed, for the Edo-period corral walls, the Matsuzaki pit dwellings, and the Spiro rectangular hut anomalies), and is the most reusable structural finding of this analysis for template purposes.

A second, distinct reasoning pattern appears specifically at Matsuzaki, where the paper substitutes **prospective ground-truth excavation** for citation-based mechanistic support: instead of citing a published physical model to justify an inference, the paper points forward to (and back-reports, since the paper post-dates 1993) an actual independent excavation that confirmed the radar-predicted feature locations — "Several undiscovered anomalies were also correctly identified during the more recent excavations in 1993." This is the paper's strongest-confidence interpretive mode: not "believed to be," but a flat corroborated-fact statement, because independent physical excavation (not just pattern-matching) supplied the confirmation.

## 11. Reusable Section Templates for Future Authors

Each template below is modeled directly on this paper's structure, phrasing patterns, and hedging style. Bracketed placeholders `[LIKE THIS]` mark what a future author must fill in. A short writing instruction follows each template.

### 11.1 Title
```
[GEOPHYSICAL METHOD] [KEY OUTPUT/PRODUCT] in [DOMAIN/APPLICATION CONTEXT]
```
*Instruction:* Keep it short (Goodman et al. used 6 words). Lead with the technique + its specific output product (here: "Time Slices"), not the site names — this paper never names a site in its title, even though it covers three.

### 11.2 Abstract (single paragraph, ~50–70 words, 3–4 sentences)
```
[DATA PRODUCT] of [GEOPHYSICAL METHOD] data are developed/derived from [ACQUISITION GEOMETRY, e.g. "closely spaced profiles"] collected at [SITE TYPE(S)] in [REGION(S)/COUNTRY-LEVEL LOCATIONS]. The [DATA PRODUCT] is/are used to [REMOTELY SENSE / DETECT / MAP] [FEATURE TYPE(S)] at [BRIEF SITE LIST, NO DETAIL]. The [METHODOLOGY / PROCESSING APPROACH] for [CREATING/PRODUCING THE DATA PRODUCT] and [an honest statement of interpretive limitations] are presented.
```
*Instruction:* Do not put numeric results or named-site conclusions in the abstract — this paper's abstract is a scope statement, not a results summary. Always include an explicit limitations clause in the final sentence; it signals scientific honesty and sets up the Conclusions' "defend + extend" pattern.

### 11.3 Introduction (3+ paragraphs, present tense for general claims, past/present-perfect for lit review)
```
Paragraph 1 — General technique framing:
One of the most useful [presentations/products] of [METHOD] data sets collected [ACQUISITION GEOMETRY] is to [KEY VALUE PROPOSITION]. [Explain what the visualization/output allows the reader to see or do.] The [product] can be [created/generated] at [VARIABLE PARAMETER] to show [FEATURE TYPE] at a specified [PARAMETER] across a surveyed site.

Paragraph 2 — Domain/audience justification:
In [DOMAIN] studies, the unique requirements of the [END USER/CUSTOMER]—usually a [PROFESSIONAL TYPE] with [RELEVANT BACKGROUND OR LACK THEREOF]—required an easy way to [ASSIMILATE/USE] the [GEOPHYSICAL] analysis into a form useful for their own [RESEARCH/WORKFLOW]. [Explain what accelerated or motivated the paper's approach.]

Paragraph 3 — Literature positioning + gap:
Several recent papers ([CITATION LIST]) have presented [RELATED PRIOR RESULTS], and [OTHER VENUE TYPE, e.g. conference papers] (e.g. [CITATION LIST]) indicate that [BROADER TREND] is beginning to be practiced. None the less, [THE PAPER'S SPECIFIC TECHNIQUE] has still yet to become a widely practiced—and a regular [ANALYSIS TYPE]—technique by the [METHOD] user.

Paragraph 4 — Roadmap:
In this paper the method for [CREATING THE DATA PRODUCT] and [its application/their application] to studying [N] [DOMAIN] sites is presented. In one case, [SPECIFIC VALIDATION MOVE, e.g. "the site plans based on the survey are compared with excavation/ground-truth site plans"].
```
*Instruction:* Justify the paper by a *practical/audience* gap (end users can't use raw output) as well as/instead of a purely scientific gap. Frame prior work cumulatively ("beginning to be practiced," "still yet to become widely practiced") rather than critically. Always end the Introduction with an explicit one-to-two sentence roadmap.

For the sites surveyed in this study, a [VALUE + UNIT] interval was believed to be sufficient to [map the desired targets]. Data were recorded [continuously/at intervals] and a marker placed in the data every [UNIT].

Paragraph 2 — Resampling / correction / parameter definition:
Because [SOURCE OF SAMPLING VARIABILITY], the data were later resampled to a constant [VALUE] using software. [CORRECTION TYPE(S)] were applied. [DEFINE THE CORE DATA-REDUCTION PARAMETER IN ONE SENTENCE, e.g. "Averages of the squared amplitude of X over a [spatial] window and a [depth/time] window were used as the [output] parameter to be mapped."]

Paragraph 3 — Justification for parameter choices, with an aside contrasting alternative approaches from an adjacent field:
For our [DOMAIN] applications, [CHOICE] was necessary to create meaningful [output]. (As often is the case in [ADJACENT FIELD] data processes, [CONTRASTING APPROACH] can create higher-resolution data that requires less [PROCESSING STEP]. We have found it useful, in [SPECIFIC DATA TYPE], to apply [TECHNIQUE].)

Paragraph 4 — Gridding/interpolation parameters + explicit caveat:
[GRIDDING METHOD] were used ... with a search radius of [VALUE] and a[n] [WEIGHTING SCHEME] of [VALUE]. A problem with [STEP] is that [SPECIFIC DISTORTION, e.g. anomaly size inflation] will tend to occur. This problem could be reduced, however, by [MITIGATION].

Paragraph 6 — Named, flagged open problem + practical consequence for interpretation:

[Interpretation, tied to an independent corroborating source where possible, e.g. historical aerial photographs, known land-use history.]

Paragraph 3 — Deep-slice / primary-target interpretation (the 8-step chain):
On the [deepest/target] slice, [interval], a [SHAPE DESCRIPTOR] anomaly is imaged. The [anomaly] is approximately [SIZE] in [UNIT]. One [profile/cross-section] taken across [feature] is shown in Figure [N+1]. [Name any recognizable pattern, e.g. "bow-tie"], citing prior mechanistic work if available. [Explicit caveat about confounds, e.g. equipment/aperture dependence of the pattern.]

Paragraph 4 (optional) — Secondary feature class on the same slice:
Several other [FEATURE TYPE] were imaged on the [slice]. These [features] are believed to be [INTERPRETATION], built/created during [PERIOD], using [CONSTRUCTION METHOD]. The radar patterns imaged from [technique] are consistent with this [construction method] because [SPECIFIC PATTERN-TO-MECHANISM LINK].
```
*Instruction:* Always give archaeological/cultural context *before* showing data — the reader needs vocabulary and stakes before interpreting an image. Use the hedge "is/are believed to be" for every archaeological inference not independently confirmed by excavation; reserve flat factual statements ("were found... and corroborated by...") only for claims that *are* independently ground-truthed.

### 11.6 Methodological-Extension Section (e.g. "Topographically corrected [PRODUCT]" — optional, use when demonstrating a processing refinement on a further site)
```
Paragraph 1 — New site + why the refinement matters here:
One example of applying [REFINEMENT] to a [DATA TYPE] was done for the [SITE NAME], [LOCATION]. The site is [famous/significant] for [ARCHAEOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE], whose [inhabitants/occupants] [HISTORICAL FATE]. [REFINEMENT-SPECIFIC PRODUCT] across the site were made every [INTERVAL] from [REFERENCE] (Figure [N]). Some inherent errors will occur by this assumption, however, the [uncorrected alternative] provides a significantly less desirable data set for interpretative purposes.

Paragraph 2 — Structural/topographic behavior with depth:
As the [FEATURE, e.g. mound] is [cut/imaged] deeper, the [horizontal extent/other property] of the anomalies will also indicate [RELATED PHYSICAL PROPERTY]. [Explain any systematic amplitude/quality change with depth and its physical cause.]

Paragraph 3 — Deep-feature interpretation with appropriate hedging:
At deeper [slices/levels], [FEATURE PATTERN] begin to emerge. Several interpretations suggest that [PATTERN] may be related to [ARCHAEOLOGICAL HYPOTHESIS], [with explicit uncertainty language: "may be," "suggest," "sequentially... or..."].

Paragraph 4 — Closing methodological verdict:

```
Sentence/paragraph 1 — General reaffirmation, generalized beyond the specific sites:

[Own citation] also looked at [RELATED REFINEMENT]. This is particularly important in the [METHOD] because [REASON]. [REFINEMENT] are usually the only proper way to display these data sets in a meaningful way. *Instruction:* Do not simply restate results. ## 12. Summary of the Paper's Overall Structural Signature

The paper's shape is: **Abstract (scope promise) → Introduction (general rationale + audience-need justification + literature-maturity gap) → Methods-as-subsection (full acquisition + processing pipeline, explicit about omissions, one named open problem) → three parallel "Application to [Site]" sections of increasing methodological sophistication (basic discovery → ground-truth validation → processing refinement/topographic correction) → Conclusions (generalize + defend against a real objection + point to further refinements) → Acknowledgements (named local contacts + software citation) → References (author-year, mixing journals/textbooks/conference proceedings).**

The throughline that a future author should imitate is the **repeated, hedged, evidence-layered anomaly-interpretation formula** ("X is imaged" → "X is approximately [size]" → "X is believed to be [interpretation]" → cross-validate with a second view/citation/excavation → flag confounds), applied consistently across all three case-study sites and scaled in confidence level according to how much independent corroboration (aerial photos, known construction methods, or actual excavation) is available for each specific claim.
