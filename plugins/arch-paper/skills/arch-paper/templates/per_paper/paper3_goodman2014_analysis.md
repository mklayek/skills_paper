# Paper Analysis: Goodman (2014) — "The Search for Subterranean Kofun Burial Chambers on the Island of Kyushu, Japan using Ground Penetrating Radar"

---

## 0. Source Identification & Framing

This is **not a full journal-format IMRaD paper** — it is a **short, figure-driven conference/field-report chapter** (9 pages, ~2,600 words of running text, 5 figures, no tables, no equations, 5 references). It reads like a site-report chapter from a proceedings volume or an edited archaeological-geophysics volume (the style, length, and reference to Goodman & Piro's own 2013 Springer book strongly suggest this is a chapter contributed to a compilation on archaeological prospection case studies, in the vein of *Proceedings of the ICAP* or a regional Japanese archaeology yearbook).

**Why this matters for the template**: this paper is the *minimal / compressed* end of the archaeological-GPR paper spectrum. Where a full journal article would expand each of these paragraphs into its own subsectioned block (Site Setting, Materials & Methods, Data Acquisition, Processing, Results, Interpretation, Discussion, Conclusions), Goodman (2014) fuses them into **three continuous, unlabeled narrative movements** dressed under only three headers: `Introduction`, `GPR Survey`, `GPR Vector Imaging`. This is a deliberate register: informal, narrative, first-encounter storytelling for a semi-general archaeological readership, backed by dense figure evidence rather than dense prose. The template must capture BOTH the compressed short-report register (as seen here) AND note where each compressed paragraph would "unpack" into full journal sections, since the reusable template is meant to serve longer manuscripts too.

## 2. Section & Subsection Hierarchy

Exact headings, in order, as they appear (verbatim, no numbering scheme is used anywhere in this paper):

```
[Title block — title, 4 authors with affiliations, Keywords line]

Introduction
   (2 unlabeled paragraphs)

GPR Survey
   (5 unlabeled paragraphs, one of which is functionally a "Materials and
    Methods" paragraph, the rest are "Results/Interpretation" case narratives)

GPR Vector Imaging
   (2 unlabeled paragraphs — functions as a second, specialized Methods +
    Results sub-study nested inside the same paper)

Acknowledgements
   (1 sentence — software credit only)

References
   (5 references, unnumbered author-year list)

[Figures 1–5, each on its own page, image + caption, placed AFTER references]
```

**Key structural observations:**

- **No numbering scheme at all** — no "1. Introduction", no "1.1", no Roman numerals. All headers are bare bolded/plain text, flush left, no decimal numbering. This is typical of short chapters where the numbering is supplied by the parent volume's table of contents, not the chapter itself.
- **Only 3 top-level headers govern ~4 pages of body text** — a very shallow hierarchy (depth = 1, no subsections at all). Compare to a full journal IMRaD paper, which would have 6–10 top-level sections and often 2–3 levels of subsection numbering.
- **Figures are physically separated from their in-text citation** — all 5 figures are placed at the *end* of the document (pp. 5–9), each on its own page, AFTER the References section — not inline near their first citation as in a modern typeset journal. This is a manuscript/proceedings-submission convention (figures-at-end is common for camera-ready chapter submissions where the editor inserts figures during layout) rather than a final-typeset convention. **Template implication**: when reusing this structure for a new paper, decide up front whether the target venue wants figures inline (modern journal norm) or batched at the end (proceedings/submission norm) — the two demand different in-text referencing rhythm (see §6).
- **"GPR Survey" doubles as both Methods and Results** — there is no clean separation between "how we collected/processed the data" and "what we found." The methods paragraph (soil conditions, frequencies, processing steps) is sandwiched between narrative results (Sakamotonoue chamber discovery, Figure 3 depth-slice anomaly cluster). This fusion is a deliberate compression technique for short-format papers: readers get method context *just before* the finding it enabled, rather than reading a full methods block up front.
- **"GPR Vector Imaging" is a self-contained mini-paper within the paper** — it has its own implicit method ("special analysis is made to adjust the radargrams...for roll, pitch and yaw") and its own implicit result (Mound #202's square burial pit measurement, moat geometry, absence of subsurface voids). This is the template pattern for **papers that showcase two methods/case-sites under one title**: give the second method/site its own header at the same hierarchical level as the general survey section, even though it's really a subsection of "Results."

## 3. Section-by-Section Deep Analysis

### 3.1 Introduction (functions as Abstract + Historical/Archaeological Context + Site Justification)

**Purpose and writing logic**: Because there is no formal Abstract, the Introduction must do triple duty: (1) hook the reader with a narrative-historical scene-set, (2) establish the archaeological stakes (national treasure, imperial lineage, scale/rarity of the site), and (3) narrow from "the region" to "the specific research problem" (many mounds destroyed/lost, GPR is a way to recover the lost record). This is the **funnel structure**: wide historical scope → geographic narrowing → the loss/gap that justifies the GPR work → sets up "GPR Survey" as the payoff section.

**Paragraph organization and flow** (2 paragraphs):
- **Paragraph 1** — deep-time historical/archaeological scene-setting. Opens 1700 years in the past, describes funerary customs, geography (plateau, valleys, mountains — cites Figure 1 immediately), narrows to "Miyazaki prefecture," names the specific mounds (Osaho, Mesaho), and closes on their imperial/sacred significance ("entomb the great grandparents of the first emperor of Japan").
- **Paragraph 2** — problem statement dressed as narrative: site scale (50 km from Miyazaki city, 350+ mounds, "unique in Japan"), then the *loss narrative* — mounds destroyed by farming, weathering disguising their existence, "no knowledge of their existence might ever be recovered" — and finally introduces the taxonomy of burial types (shallow surface burials vs. secondary subterranean chambers, up to 3–4 chambers per mound) that the GPR survey will later be shown detecting.

**Opening sentence pattern** (quote verbatim):
> "Around seventeen hundred years ago in the southern regions on the island of Kyushu funerary customs began to take hold."

> "Located about 50 km from the bustling city of Miyazaki, the Saitobaru tombs are unique in Japan in that such a high concentration of burial mounds that total over 350 could be found in one location..."

Pattern: **time/place adverbial phrase fronting the sentence**, verb of emergence/location ("began to take hold," "Located about... are unique"), THEN the subject clause. This defers the grammatical subject and creates a scene-establishing, almost cinematic opening rhythm — very different from a journal Introduction's typical opener ("GPR has been widely used to..." or "This study presents...").

**Typical content that belongs here** (per this paper's model):
- Deep historical/cultural context of the burial tradition (dates, customs, mound typology)
- Naming and significance of the specific site/monuments (imperial connection, National Treasure status)
- Scale statement (number of mounds, area)
- The loss/damage narrative — what has been destroyed, why (agrarian development), and why that's a problem worth solving
- Taxonomy of the archaeological feature classes that will recur later (surface burials vs. subterranean chambers) — this is a **forward-loading device**: define the vocabulary here so later sections ("shallow burial of 1-2 meters," "intact subterranean chambers") don't need re-explanation.

**Verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases (voice)**:
1. "Large earthen burial mounds of varying shapes and sizes began to appear on an elevated plateau surrounded by gradual valleys and nearby flatlands that were bounded by tall mountains (Figure 1)."
2. "Folklore states that the Osaho and Mesaho mounds which are several hundred meters long and several 10s of meters tall entomb the great grandparents of the first emperor of Japan in the 7th century AD"
3. "The level of destruction of some mounds is complete and no knowledge of their existence might ever be recovered."
4. "The shallow elevation on some mounds along with weathering disguised the existence that a burial once existed and the area might then have been altered for farming production."
5. "Upwards of 3-4 intact subterranean chambers beneath a single mound can be found in some locations intact."

**No explicit "literature review" subsection** — the one prior-work citation in this whole introduction area is deferred to the start of "GPR Survey" ("GPR surveys were first initiated at Saitobaru in 1993... Some of the earlier surveying successes also included discovery of buried moats... [2,3]"). This shows the template convention: **in a short case-study report, cite prior work only where it directly sets up the present survey's continuity/track record, not as a standalone lit-review block.**

### 3.2 "GPR Survey" (functions as Site/Geological Setting + Materials & Methods + Data Acquisition + Processing + Results + Interpretation, fused)

This is the paper's largest and most information-dense section — 5 paragraphs doing the work that a full journal article would spread across 5–6 separate headed sections. Below, each paragraph is decomposed by its *functional* role even though none of them carry sub-headers in the source.

#### 3.2.a Paragraph 1 — Site Track Record / Framing (functions as "prior work at this site" + problem restatement)

**Purpose**: Establish continuity ("still being done today"), name the twin research goals (subterranean chambers AND shallow surface burials), and cite the single most significant prior discovery at the site (the reconstructed triangular Osaho mound edge) as a proof-of-method vignette before describing the current methodology.

**Opening sentence**:
> "GPR surveys were first initiated at Saitobaru in 1993 and are still being done today in searching the 54 hectare site for lost subterranean chamber burials as well as to discover shallow burials on the tops of the earthen mounds."

Pattern: states **when** the survey program began, its **duration/currency** ("still being done today"), the **site size** (54 hectares — a concrete quantifier), and the **twin objectives**, all in one sentence. This "one sentence = full survey-program abstract" compression is characteristic of the whole paper.

**Characteristic sentence (method teaser embedded in a discovery anecdote)**:
> "Using specialized overlay analysis where images at varying depths are used to synthesize important reflections across a whole band of depth ranges, a single image showing the shape of the destroyed triangular bottom of the Osaho mound could be reconstructed [2]."

Note the self-citation to Goodman & Piro (2013) — the technique ("overlay analysis") is named, minimally defined in-line (not a full methods description), and immediately followed by a citation pointing the reader to the fuller technical treatment elsewhere. **Template pattern**: when a technique has been fully described in a prior publication (esp. the author's own book/paper), a short-format paper only needs a one-clause functional definition + citation, not a re-derivation.

#### 3.2.b Paragraph 2 — Materials and Methods / Data Acquisition / Processing (the paper's only concentrated "Methods" paragraph)

**Purpose**: Compress instrument parameters, survey geometry, ground conditions, and the full processing chain into a single paragraph. This is the clearest example in the paper of the **"methods-as-a-list-inside-a-paragraph"** pattern common to short-format geophysics chapters — sentences are short, declarative, and enumerate parameters without elaborating on why (that justification, if any, lives in the cited references, not here).

**Opening sentence**:
> "GPR surveys employing varying frequencies from 200, 270 and 500 MHz have been expedited."

**Content inventory** (everything a Materials & Methods section must cover, shown here compressed into ~180 words):
- Antenna frequencies used: 200, 270, 500 MHz, with a rationale clause for using lower frequencies on larger mounds ("ancillary information is desired such as stratigraphic information which the lower frequency antenna are well suited to")
- Ground conditions affecting penetration: "highly resistive volcanic soils," penetration depth ("upwards of 200ns... even 500 MHz")
- Survey geometry: line spacing ("50cm intervals," "25cm on the tops of burial portions")
- Full processing chain named as a flat list: "dewowing, bandpass filtering, Kirchoff migration and Hilbert transform"
- Post-processing / slice generation: spatial resampling into "averaged squared amplitudes in consecutive overlapped windows... approximately 30 cm thick in depth and 0.25m in length," gridding via "simple inverse distance algorithm," output "2D depth slices [4] at 5 cm pixels"
- 3D volume generation: "interpolation of normalized 2D depth slices, where each map has independent colorization transforms applied" — and an explanatory aside on *why* this method is used (auto-gain benefit) rather than gaining the raw radargram

**Characteristic sentences**:
1. "Penetration depths with GPR are quite good in the highly resistive volcanic soils at Saitobaru and upwards of 200ns of penetration can be easily achieved with even 500 MHz antennae."
2. "Radargram signal processes included dewowing, bandpass filtering, Kirchoff migration and Hilbert transform."
3. "The interpolation of the normalized 2D images into 3D volumes effectively uses the automatic gaining in the images – rather than in the radargram preprocessing - to generate auto gained volumes versus depth."

**Writing logic**: notice there is **no justification of WHY these particular processing steps were chosen** (no "we selected Kirchoff migration because...") — the paragraph is a flat enumeration, trusting either the reader's domain familiarity or the cited companion volume [4] to supply the rationale. This is a hallmark of the **compressed technical register**: name the operations, cite the fuller source, move on to results. A full journal Methods section would instead justify each choice against data characteristics (noise, target depth, antenna ringing) — the template should offer BOTH a compressed list-style variant (as here) and an expanded justified variant for longer papers.

#### 3.2.c Paragraph 3 — Case Vignette #1: Sakamotonoue Chamber Discovery (Results + ground-truth/Validation, narrated as a discovery story)

**Purpose**: This is the paper's central proof-of-concept anecdote — GPR detection immediately followed by excavation confirmation. It is written as a **discovery narrative**, not a results table: site name → radargram description → interpreted feature → *[Figure 2 citation]* → excavation outcome → the human/material findings that validate the anomaly as a real burial.

**Opening sentence**:
> "One site area surveyed in the western areas in Saitobaru call Sakamotonoue, a series of tunnel burial chambers were discovered with GPR and then later excavated (Figure 2)."

(Note: this sentence, as extracted, has a grammatical slip — "call Sakamotonoue" instead of "called Sakamotonoue" — the template should flag that even published/expert-authored short-format chapters can carry minor grammatical roughness; this is informative for calibrating how polished a case-study report register needs to be, but a template should still recommend clean grammar.)

**Validation logic embedded directly in the narrative** (this is the key move to extract for the template's "Interpretation Methodology" section, §10 below):
> "Excavations inside the subterranean chambers revealed an intact human skeleton along with pottery, swords, bronze mirrors and golden earrings."

The anomaly → excavation → artifact-list sequence *is* the validation. There is no separate "ground-truthing" or "discussion of anomaly confidence" section — the artifact list stands in as proof that the GPR interpretation was correct. This is a **direct empirical validation narrative**, the strongest form of GPR-archaeology proof (survey prediction, then dig confirms it), and the paper leans on it heavily rather than on statistical/quantitative anomaly-confidence measures.

**Characteristic sentences**:
1. "The radar profile shows strong reflections from the ceilings of 2 intact chambers located in close proximity to one another."
2. "The chamber ceilings appear around 2.5 m below the ground surface."
3. "Using the entire dataset, a generalized isosurface rendering of the 75% strongest reflectors in the normalized volume could be used to show the 3D orientation, size and extent of the burial chambers."
4. "Prior to excavation of the site, air samples as well as a camera were inserted into the void space of the chambers to record the constituents of the ancient air before the burial was sealed in the 6th century AD."

The 4th sentence is a striking example of **archaeological-narrative color woven into a geophysics paper** — a detail (pre-excavation air sampling, 6th-century sealing) that has nothing to do with the GPR method itself but grounds the reader in the site's archaeological gravity. **Template implication**: case-study GPR papers earn reader interest by alternating strictly technical sentences with such human/archaeological-interest sentences — a deliberate register-mixing, not an accident.

#### 3.2.d Paragraph 4 — Geological/Site Setting (the ash-soil explanation) — placed AFTER the case result, not before

**Purpose**: Explain *why* this site is geologically suited to subterranean chamber construction (and, implicitly, why GPR works so well there — highly resistive volcanic ash gives strong, clean reflections and good penetration). Notably, this "Site/Geological Setting" content — which in a full journal paper would appear near the very front, before Methods — is placed HERE, after the first major result, functioning as a **retrospective explanation** for why the preceding discovery was geophysically and archaeologically possible.

**Opening sentence**:
> "The soil structure that makes Saitobaru suitable to the construction of subterranean chamber is the high concentration of ash in the soil."

**Characteristic sentences**:
1. "Mt Kirishima which is still an active volcano today had actually last erupted on January 29, 2011 and preceded the Tohoku earthquake precariously by less than 6 weeks."
2. "The deeply layered ash is hard packed and is a perfect natural and strong material that can be easily carved into and at the same time is able to support the overburden weight of soil above any excavated chambers."
3. "The depth to the ceiling of the burial chambers can be just 1-2 several meters below the original ground surface and have sizes as large as a walk-in closet into today's modern houses."
4. "Heavier farming equipment employed today in Japan occasionally fall into one of these ancient chambers that once existed below a destroyed burial mound causing injuries."

The "walk-in closet" simile and the modern farming-equipment hazard anecdote are two more instances of vivid, reader-accessible analogy dropped into an otherwise technical narrative — a recurring Goodman stylistic signature (see §4).

**Template implication**: this is the paper's proof that **Site/Geological Setting content does not have to be front-loaded** — it can be deployed exactly where it is needed to explain a result, which for a short/compressed case-study format is often more effective and less redundant than a separate upfront section.

#### 3.2.e Paragraph 5 — Case Vignette #2: Figure 3 depth-slice cluster over Google Earth (Results + Interpretation, unconfirmed by excavation — contrast with Paragraph 3)

**Purpose**: A second results narrative, explicitly weaker in evidentiary strength than the Sakamotonoue case — this cluster of anomalies is interpreted as "the presence of intact subterranean chambers" but (as told here) has NOT yet been excavated/confirmed. This paragraph therefore models the **"probable but unconfirmed" interpretation register** — important to capture for a template since not every GPR anomaly in a paper will have a dig to back it up.

**Opening sentence**:
> "Shown in Figure 3 is a georeferenced depth slice from 1.1-1.4 m below the ground surface overlain on a Google Earth photo of an area in the southeastern portion of Saitobaru."

**Characteristic sentences**:
1. "This area is flanked by several keyhole shaped mounds that were partially excavated in the Taisho period over 100 years ago followed by more thorough excavations that were completed recently."
2. "The GPR surveys in the northern portion of this site show an area with a cluster of several distinct and strong reflections that indicate the presence of intact subterranean chambers beneath the ground here."
3. "(Because of the near shallow presence of some of the subterranean chamber to the ground surface, some of them can actually be discovered by tapping ones foot on the ground to hear a subsurface echo of the ground at these locations)."

Sentence 3 (a full parenthetical aside) is a lovely example of folk/field-method color — a low-tech cross-check method (foot-tapping for hollow echoes) mentioned almost as an anecdotal footnote. **Template implication**: parenthetical asides are a legitimate device in this register for adding corroborating "sanity check" observations that don't merit their own paragraph.

### 3.3 "GPR Vector Imaging" (a self-contained Methods + Results sub-study — the paper's most "technical" section)

**Purpose and writing logic**: This section pivots from narrative-driven storytelling to the paper's most overtly *technical/methodological* content — describing a specific processing innovation (topography-driven roll/pitch/yaw correction of radargrams, i.e., "vector imaging") and demonstrating it on a single named target (Burial Mound #202). It functions as a **method spotlight nested within the case study** — common in short papers that want to showcase one specific technical contribution distinctly from the general survey narrative.

**Paragraph organization** (2 paragraphs):
- **Paragraph 1**: states the general problem (topographic slope distorts radargram/volume geometry unless corrected), names the correction method (surface-normal-derived post-processing), states its one key assumption plainly, and flags a side-effect problem it introduces (radargram crossover/intersection in the corrected 3D space) — with an explicit forward-reference to Figure 4 as the illustration of that side-effect.
- **Paragraph 2**: applies the method to the specific case (Mound #202 keyhole tomb), reports the measured feature (5 m square burial pit at the 103 cm slice), reports the negative result at depth (no subsurface void chambers below 6.4 m — meaning this mound only had a shallow surface burial), and closes with a chronological/typological interpretation (later Kofun-period dating inferred from keyhole-bottom elevation pattern).

**Opening sentence**:
> "When the sites surveyed with GPR have significant topographic changes, special analysis is made to adjust the radargrams and 3D volumes to account for roll, pitch and yaw of the antenna on the site."

Pattern: **conditional/circumstantial clause fronting** ("When X happens, Y is done") — this is the paper's standard method-introduction template, distinct from the narrative-opening pattern used in the Introduction (§3.1) and the anecdote-opening pattern used in the case vignettes (§3.2.c, §3.2.e). Three distinct opening-sentence registers coexist in this one short paper — see §4 for the full taxonomy.

**Characteristic sentences**:
1. "Although tilt meters are not currently placed on the equipment for real time monitoring for most applications today, GPR post processing using the surface normal determined from the topography is utilized to correct the data [2, 5]."
2. "The only assumption incorporated assumes that antenna is orientated flat and in absolute contact with local mound surface during GPR profiling in the field." — **this is the paper's single explicit "limitations/assumptions" statement** — worth flagging because it is the *only* place in the whole paper where a methodological assumption is stated as a caveat.
3. "Some unique problems can happen when roll/pitch/yaw corrections are made - radargrams can intersect and crossover regions where other nearby radargrams are projected." — this is a **method-limitation-acknowledged-through-a-figure** move: rather than writing a "Discussion of Limitations" section, the limitation is illustrated directly via Figure 4's caption and cross-referenced here.
4. "Burial mound #202 is of a later date in the Kofun period and is identified by the nearly taller elevations of the keyhole bottom compared with the central round burial top." — this is the paper's **closing interpretive sentence**, doubling as an implicit "Conclusion" for this sub-case (typological/chronological inference drawn purely from GPR-derived morphology, no excavation cited for this particular mound).

**This section is the paper's de facto "Discussion of methodological caveats"** — despite carrying no such header, it performs that function by directly naming an assumption, naming a side-effect artifact, and pointing to the figure that shows it, all inline.

**Full text (verbatim, complete)**:

## 4. Writing Style Analysis

### 4.1 Tense usage by section

| Section | Dominant tense | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Past tense (historical narrative: "began to take hold," "were conscripted") + present tense for ongoing states ("today," "is called," "have survived") | Classic historical-narrative mixed-tense: past for events, present for facts/conditions that still hold |
| GPR Survey (methods paragraph) | Present perfect / passive present ("have been expedited," "are recorded," "included") mixed with simple past for what WAS done ("Profiles for most sites were recorded at 50cm intervals") | Methods described as completed actions in passive voice, standard for a methods report |
| GPR Survey (case vignettes) | Simple past for the excavation narrative ("were discovered," "revealed an intact human skeleton") + present tense for describing what the figure/data currently shows ("The radar profile shows strong reflections," "appear around 2.5 m") | Mixed past/present is used precisely: past = what happened (survey, dig); present = what the data/figure shows NOW to the reader |
| GPR Vector Imaging | Present tense for method description ("is made," "is utilized"), past tense for the case application ("could be measured and found to be") | Method-in-general = present; specific case application = past/modal ("could be") |
| Acknowledgements | Simple past ("were accomplished using") | Standard |

### 4.2 Voice

- **Predominantly passive voice** throughout methods content: "Profiles for most sites were recorded," "Radargram signal processes included," "GPR surveys employing varying frequencies... have been expedited," "special analysis is made." This is the conventional geophysics-methods passive register (no "we did X" — data/processes are the grammatical subject, not the researchers).
- **Narrative sections shift toward active/impersonal-subject voice** where the *site or feature* becomes the subject: "Large earthen burial mounds... began to appear," "the Saitobaru tombs are unique," "Mt Kirishima... had actually last erupted." The mounds and the landscape are grammatically animated as active narrative subjects — a stylistic device that reinforces the "search/discovery" framing of the title.
- **First person is never used** — no "we," "our," or "I" anywhere in the extracted text, despite the narrative warmth of the prose. This is notable: the paper achieves a storytelling register WITHOUT first-person voice, purely through subject choice (site/mound/radar-image as actor) and modal/anecdotal sentence construction.

### 4.3 Sentence length and variety

- Sentences are frequently **long and multi-clausal**, often 40–60+ words, built from chained relative clauses and appositives (e.g., the 45-word opening sentence of paragraph 2, Introduction, stacking three subordinate/relative clauses about the tombs' size, location, and imperial connection).
- Short declarative sentences are reserved for **key technical facts or emphatic closing statements** within a paragraph, e.g., "The chamber ceilings appear around 2.5 m below the ground surface." — a deliberate rhythm break after a long descriptive sentence, functioning almost like a topic-sentence "reveal."
- Parenthetical asides `(Figure 1)`, `(Figure 2)`, and the longer parenthetical about foot-tapping for hollow echoes, are used liberally — these keep figure citations unobtrusive (embedded, not framed as "as shown in Figure X, ...") while also allowing digressive color commentary without breaking the main sentence flow.

### 4.4 Connectors and transitions

- Paragraph-opening transitions are mostly **temporal/locative**, not logical-argumentative: "Recognized as a Japanese National Treasure..." / "One site area surveyed in the western areas..." / "Shown in Figure 3 is..." / "When the sites surveyed with GPR have significant topographic changes..." — very few "However," "Therefore," "In contrast," or "Moreover" style logical connectors. This paper builds momentum through **sequential storytelling** (site A, then site feature B, then method C) rather than through argumentative logic-chaining. This is a clear marker of the case-study-narrative register versus a hypothesis-testing argumentative register.
- The one clear causal/contrastive connector found: "Although tilt meters are not currently placed on the equipment for real time monitoring for most applications today, GPR post processing using the surface normal... is utilized to correct the data" — an "Although X, Y" concession structure, used exactly once, at the one point where the paper needs to acknowledge an equipment limitation while asserting the workaround still works.

### 4.5 How the archaeological/historical narrative is woven into the GPR technical content

This is the defining stylistic signature of the whole paper and the most important thing for the reusable template to capture. The weaving pattern is:

1. **Historical/cultural framing always precedes the technical payoff, never follows it as an afterthought.** The Introduction is 100% archaeological/historical narrative with zero GPR-technical content — GPR is not even mentioned by its method until the very last words of the Introduction's implicit setup, and formally introduced only at the "GPR Survey" header.
2. **Concrete artifact/material lists are used as the emotional and evidentiary climax of technical results** — "an intact human skeleton along with pottery, swords, bronze mirrors and golden earrings" is the single most vivid sentence in the entire paper, and it is placed immediately after the technical description of the radar anomaly, functioning as both scientific validation AND narrative payoff simultaneously.
3. **Numeric/scale facts are consistently paired with an accessible analogy** — "54 hectare site," "over 350" mounds, "several hundred meters long," chambers "as large as a walk-in closet." This habit of shadowing every hard number with a human-scale comparison is a repeated device (at least 4 instances) and should be templated as a deliberate technique: *state the number, then immediately anchor it to something a general reader can picture.*
4. **Danger/human-interest asides are used to keep stakes tangible** — the farm-equipment-falls-into-chamber anecdote, the foot-tapping detection method, the pre-excavation air-sampling detail — none of these are strictly necessary to the GPR argument, but all of them humanize the abstract "subsurface reflection" into a lived, current, consequential reality.
5. **Chronological/typological interpretation is the paper's implicit "so-what"** — both major case sections end not on a GPR-technical note but on an archaeological-typological conclusion (Sakamotonoue: burial contents identify a 6th-century sealing; Mound #202: keyhole-bottom elevation pattern identifies "a later date in the Kofun period"). GPR's role is instrumental — it exists to answer archaeological-chronological questions, and the paper's structure keeps returning the reader to that end goal rather than resting on the geophysical finding itself.

## 6. Figure Integration (this paper's most distinctive feature)

### 6.1 Placement convention

All 5 figures are placed **after the References section**, each occupying its own full page (image + caption only, minimal other content), in strict numerical order (Figure 1 → Figure 2 → Figure 3 → Figure 4 → Figure 5). This is the "figures-at-end" manuscript convention, as opposed to "figures-inline-near-first-citation." **Template guidance**: if the target venue/journal uses inline figure placement (most modern journals), the template should still preserve the *density and caption style* below but instruct the writer to place each figure immediately after its first in-text citation paragraph.

### 6.2 In-text citation style

Figures are cited **parenthetically, inline, without a framing clause**, e.g.:
- "...an elevated plateau surrounded by gradual valleys and nearby flatlands that were bounded by tall mountains **(Figure 1)**."
- "...a series of tunnel burial chambers were discovered with GPR and then later excavated **(Figure 2)**."
- "Shown in **Figure 3** is a georeferenced depth slice from 1.1-1.4 m below the ground surface overlain on a Google Earth photo..." — this is the ONE instance where the figure is fronted as the grammatical subject of the sentence ("Shown in Figure X is...") rather than cited parenthetically at the sentence's end — used deliberately when the figure IS the primary subject being introduced (a new depth-slice result), not a supporting illustration of a claim already stated.
- "This can be seen in **Figure 4** where vector imaging of the radargrams projected for Saitobaru burial mound #202 are shown."
- "Shown in **Figure 5** are images of vector corrected level depth slices from the keyhole shaped burial mound."

**Pattern summary**: two citation modes coexist — (a) trailing parenthetical `(Figure N)` appended to a sentence that stands on its own without the figure, used for supporting/illustrative figures; (b) fronted "Shown in Figure N is/are..." construction, used when the figure itself is the subject of the sentence, i.e., when introducing a new primary result. The template should preserve this distinction: **illustrative figures get parenthetical citations; primary-result figures get fronted "Shown in Figure N..." sentence constructions.**

### 6.3 Figure types present

1. **Figure 1** — regional context map (Miyazaki Prefecture location within Kyushu + rendering of the Saitobaru burial mound complex, with the two largest mounds labeled). Function: establish geographic/spatial context, orient the reader before any GPR content appears.
2. **Figure 2** — composite figure: radargram profile + isosurface 3D rendering + excavation photograph, all three combined to tell the "detection → confirmation" story in one figure. Function: the paper's key validation figure, pairing raw geophysical data, processed 3D interpretation, and ground-truth photographic evidence in a single visual argument.
3. **Figure 3** — georeferenced GPR depth slice (a single depth interval, 1.1–1.4 m) draped over a Google Earth base image. Function: spatial/geographic anomaly mapping — demonstrates how GPR results are integrated with remote-sensing/GIS context rather than shown as an isolated radar image.
4. **Figure 4** — photograph of the mound + vector-corrected radargrams + level-plane 3D corrected depth slice, shown together. Function: demonstrates the specific "vector imaging" correction technique and its known artifact (radargram crossover) directly, rather than describing it purely in prose.
5. **Figure 5** — a multi-panel series of vector-corrected depth slices across a full depth range (1.03–10.33 m) plus an isosurface rendering of strong-amplitude reflectors. Function: the paper's most data-dense figure, showing the full vertical progression of the mound's subsurface structure (burial pit near-surface, moat at depth, no chambers below) in one integrated visual sequence.

**Figure type taxonomy for the template**: (1) regional/locational context map; (2) composite validation figure (radar + 3D render + excavation photo); (3) GIS-draped single depth-slice map; (4) method-demonstration figure (raw data + corrected data + artifact shown together); (5) multi-panel depth-slice progression + isosurface summary. A comprehensive archaeological-GPR case-study paper should aim to include at least one of each of these five figure archetypes.

### 6.4 Caption style — verbatim quotes

> **Figure 1.** "Map of Miyazaki Prefecture located on the southern island of Kyushu and a rendering of the Saitobaru National Burial Mounds [1]. The 2 largest mounds shown are the Osaho and Mesaho keyhole shaped burials which belong to the Japanese Imperial Family. The smaller mounds are administered by Miyazaki Prefecture."

> **Figure 2.** "A radargram profile taken at the Sakamotonoue site shows two closely spaced subterranean chamber burial reflections. An isosurface rendering shows the general size, shape and depth of these chambers which were constructed in the Kofun period. A subsequent excavation photo shows the entrance to chamber burial #2 and the interior."

> **Figure 3.** "A depth slice from at Saitobaru made at 1.1-1.4 m and overlaid on Google Earth. In the northern location of the survey area a cluster of several strong distinct anomalies indicate the presence subterranean chamber burials. The large round reflection features are reflections from different soils comprising the burials mounds."

> **Figure 4.** "Photograph of burial mound #202 at Saitobaru along with a vector corrected radargrams and level plane 3D corrected depth slice is shown. Some crossover regions where radargrams intersect with other nearby radargrams exist at a few locations on the deeper portions on mound #202 where the topographic slope is changing strongly."

> **Figure 5.** "Vector corrected 2D level plane depths slices from 1.03-10.33 m are shown for Saitobaru Kofun Burial Mound #202 along with an isosurface rendering of the stronger reflections amplitudes in the 3D volume. The size of the square burial pit on the top of the mound can be estimated from the strong – square red anomaly seen on the 1.03 meter depth slice and is about 5 meters."

**Caption structure pattern common to all 5**:
1. **Sentence 1**: states what the figure IS and where it's from (site name, data type, depth range if applicable) — purely descriptive, no interpretation yet.
2. **Sentence 2 (and sometimes 3)**: states what the figure SHOWS/MEANS — the interpretive payload (anomaly = chamber, feature = moat, measured dimension). This is where captions carry actual analytical content, not just a bare label — a caption in this paper's style is a **mini results-and-interpretation paragraph**, not a one-line label.
3. Multi-panel/composite figures (2 and 5) use one sentence per component (radargram, then isosurface, then excavation photo) — a strict "walk the reader through the figure left-to-right / component-by-component" caption structure.
4. Quantified claims appear directly in captions ("about 5 meters," "1.1-1.4 m," "1.03-10.33 m") — captions are not purely qualitative; they carry the paper's precise numeric results in several cases, meaning a reader skimming ONLY the figures and captions could reconstruct most of the paper's key findings. This is a strong argument for **figure/caption self-sufficiency** as a design goal in the template.

## 10. Interpretation Methodology — Connecting GPR Anomalies to Buried Archaeological Features

This paper offers a clear, reusable **anomaly-to-feature interpretation logic**, worth extracting explicitly for the template:

1. **Reflection strength + geometry → feature class.** "Strong reflections from the ceilings" at a consistent depth, in a spatially coherent, closed-boundary shape (visible on a depth slice or isosurface) → interpreted as a **void/chamber ceiling** (an air/soil interface produces a strong dielectric contrast, hence a strong reflection — this physical reasoning is implicit, never spelled out explicitly in the paper, which assumes reader familiarity with GPR reflection physics).
2. **Depth-slice amplitude clustering → probable chamber cluster (unconfirmed).** A "cluster of several distinct and strong reflections" at a specific depth interval (Figure 3, 1.1–1.4 m) is interpreted as indicating "the presence of intact subterranean chamber burials" — note the hedge language ("indicate the presence," not "prove" or "confirm") used when no excavation has yet validated the anomaly. This is the paper's model for **probabilistic/unconfirmed interpretation language**.
3. **Absence of deep reflections → absence of subsurface chambers, only shallow surface burial inferred.** For Mound #202, "The depth slice dataset includes reflections below 6.4 meters the ground surface, and they do not indicate any subsurface void chambers at depth, suggesting that for this mound only a shallow surface burial of 1-2 meters deep is present." This is an important interpretive move: **a negative GPR result (no anomaly) is itself interpreted as archaeologically meaningful** (mound type classification: surface burial only, vs. mound with subterranean chamber) — not treated as an uninformative null result.
4. **Anomaly shape/geometry → measurable archaeological feature dimensions.** The "square burial pit" visible on the 103 cm depth slice is directly measured from the image ("approximately 5 meters on a side") — GPR depth slices are treated as metrically reliable enough to extract feature dimensions directly, not just qualitative presence/absence.
5. **Validation hierarchy, from strongest to weakest evidence type used in this paper**:
   - **Strongest**: direct excavation confirmation with artifact recovery (Sakamotonoue — skeleton, pottery, swords, mirrors, earrings) — full physical ground-truth.
   - **Strong/historical**: prior successful reconstruction validated implicitly by the destroyed-mound-shape match to known mound morphology (Osaho triangular edge reconstruction).
   - **Moderate**: morphological/typological reasoning from GPR-derived geometry alone, cross-referenced against known regional archaeological typology (Mound #202's keyhole-bottom elevation pattern → "later date in the Kofun period," reasoning by analogy to established Kofun chronological typology, no direct dig cited).
   - **Weak/anecdotal corroboration**: folk-knowledge cross-checks mentioned in passing (foot-tapping for hollow echoes) — offered as color/plausibility support, not as rigorous validation.
   - **Unconfirmed/prospective**: anomaly clusters flagged as "indicating the presence of" chambers with no excavation yet performed (Figure 3 cluster) — explicitly hedged, positioned as a future-work implied target.

This five-tier validation hierarchy — from full excavation-confirmed ground-truth down to unconfirmed prospective anomaly — is the single most valuable interpretive-methodology takeaway from this paper for the reusable template. **A strong archaeological-GPR case-study paper should explicitly range across this hierarchy and be honest about where each described anomaly sits on it**, exactly as Goodman (2014) does (contrast the confident, fully-validated Sakamotonoue paragraph against the hedged, unconfirmed Figure 3 paragraph).

## 11. Reusable Per-Section Template with Placeholders and Writing Instructions

Below is the master template distilled from this paper, structured so it can scale from a short compressed case-study chapter (as in Goodman 2014) up to a fuller journal-length manuscript. Bracketed items are placeholders; italic notes are writing instructions.

### TITLE
```
The [Search for / Discovery of / Geophysical Investigation of] [Feature Type] at
[Site Name], [Region], [Country] using [Primary Method]
```
*Writing instruction*: use an active/narrative verb ("Search for," "Discovery of") if writing for a general-archaeology or edited-volume audience; use a neutral noun phrase ("GPR Survey of...") if writing for a strict geophysics journal. Always end with "using [Method]" to make the technique explicit and searchable.

### AUTHORS & AFFILIATIONS
```
[Lead Geophysicist/Author], [Title], [Institution], [City, Country], [website], [email]
[Site Authority Co-author 1], [Title — Curator/Director], [Museum/Site Institution]
[Site Authority Co-author 2], [Title — Archaeologist], [Museum/Site Institution]
[National/Regional Institute Co-author], [Title], [National Research Institute]
```
*Writing instruction*: pair the geophysical-method specialist (usually lead/corresponding author, only one listed with full contact info) with local site-authority co-authors (curatorial/museum) and a national-institute archaeologist — this triad signals both technical competence and legitimate site access/archaeological authority.

### KEYWORDS
```
Keywords: [culture/period + feature type], [primary geophysical method], [method category umbrella term]
```
*Writing instruction*: keep to 3–5 terms; always include one culturally/period-specific term, one specific method name, and one broader disciplinary umbrella term (e.g., "subsurface remote sensing," "archaeological prospection").

### ABSTRACT (optional in short-format chapters; MANDATORY for journal submission — add if missing)
*Writing instruction*: if the target venue requires a formal abstract (most journals do), synthesize one ~150–250 word paragraph covering: (1) site/cultural context in one sentence, (2) the archaeological problem/gap (what's unknown or lost), (3) method used and key parameters, (4) headline result(s) with at least one quantified finding, (5) significance/validation statement (e.g., "confirmed by subsequent excavation"). Goodman (2014) skips this — do not skip it for a journal-format adaptation.

### INTRODUCTION
*Purpose*: establish deep historical/cultural context, narrow to the specific site and its significance, state the archaeological loss/knowledge-gap problem, and define recurring feature-type vocabulary that later sections will use without re-explanation.

```
[Time-depth opening clause] in [region], [cultural practice] began to [emerge/take hold].
[Description of monument type: scale, form, distribution] (Figure 1).
In [modern place name], [practice] lasted until [end date] and produced [quantity] of
[feature type] that survive today. [Named largest/most significant examples] are
[superlative claim — e.g., largest, most concentrated] in [region], and because of
[reason] were [designated/protected status].

Located [distance] from [nearest city], the [site name] is unique/significant in
that [scale/density claim]. [Loss narrative: what has been damaged/destroyed, by what
process, over what timespan]. [Feature-type taxonomy: define the 2+ classes of
archaeological feature — e.g., surface burials vs. subterranean features — that later
results sections will refer back to].
```
*Writing instruction*: open with a time/place adverbial clause fronting the sentence (not "This paper presents..."). Always cite the primary context/overview figure (regional map) at the first mention of monument distribution. Close the Introduction on the taxonomy/vocabulary that Results will lean on — this is a forward-loading device, not filler.

### SITE / GEOLOGICAL SETTING
*Purpose*: explain why the site's geology/soil enables both the archaeological feature type (e.g., carved chambers) AND favorable geophysical conditions (e.g., high resistivity, strong dielectric contrast). Can be placed either up-front (journal convention) or embedded after the first major result as a retrospective explanation (Goodman's compressed-format convention — used when the "why it works" explanation is more meaningful once the reader has already seen a positive result).

```
The [soil/geological unit] that makes [site] suitable for [feature construction/GPR
detection] is [geological process/material] — [e.g., volcanic ash, alluvium, karst].
[Named geological source, e.g., a specific volcano/formation] has [ongoing/historical
activity], depositing [material] for [timespan]. This [material] is [physical property
relevant to both construction and radar propagation — e.g., "hard packed," "highly
resistive"], making it well suited to [archaeological use] while also permitting
[GPR penetration depth / signal quality benefit].
```
*Writing instruction*: always link the geological explanation to BOTH the archaeological feature's constructability AND the geophysical method's effectiveness in the same paragraph — this dual-purpose explanation is efficient and avoids a separate disconnected "geophysical setting" paragraph.

### MATERIALS AND METHODS / DATA ACQUISITION
*Purpose*: enumerate instrument, survey geometry, and ground-condition parameters. Can be written as a compressed flat list (short-format) or expanded with justification per choice (full journal format).

```
GPR surveys employed [frequency list, e.g., 200, 270, 500 MHz] [antenna type/system
name if relevant]. [Frequency-selection rationale, e.g., lower frequency for deeper
stratigraphic targets]. Ground conditions ([soil type/resistivity]) permitted
penetration depths of [X ns / X m] even with [highest frequency antenna]. Survey
lines were recorded at [X cm] intervals, [narrower spacing, X cm] over [specific
target zones, e.g., mound tops]. Processing included [flat list: dewow, bandpass
filter, migration algorithm, Hilbert transform / envelope, etc.]. [Depth-slice /
volume generation method: window thickness, spatial resampling scheme, gridding
algorithm, pixel resolution]. 3D volumes were generated via [interpolation method],
with [gain/normalization strategy and rationale].
```
*Writing instruction — SHORT FORMAT*: keep this as one dense paragraph, name each processing step without justifying it, and cite a companion reference for full technical derivation. *Writing instruction — FULL JOURNAL FORMAT*: split into "Data Acquisition" (instrument, survey geometry, ground conditions) and "Data Processing" (each processing step justified against a specific data quality issue it addresses — e.g., "Bandpass filtering (X–Y MHz) was applied to suppress low-frequency ringing evident in raw radargrams") and consider adding Table 1 (survey/processing parameter table, see §7 above) and an equation block for the depth-slice amplitude computation (see §8 above).

### RESULTS / CASE VIGNETTE (repeat this block once per major site/anomaly)
*Purpose*: narrate a single site/target's GPR result as a mini discovery story: what was surveyed, what the data shows, what it was interpreted as, and — critically — WHERE on the validation hierarchy (§10) this interpretation sits.

```
[Site/grid name] was surveyed with GPR and [feature type] were [discovered /
interpreted] (Figure N). The [radargram/depth-slice/isosurface] shows [description
of reflection: strength, shape, depth]. [Feature] appear[s] around [depth] below
the ground surface.

[IF EXCAVATION-CONFIRMED:] Excavation[s] [revealed / confirmed] [artifact/feature
inventory, named specifically — do not generalize to "various artifacts"].
[Optional: additional pre-/post-excavation methodological detail, e.g., non-invasive
sampling performed before excavation, conservation measures taken].

[IF UNCONFIRMED/PROSPECTIVE:] The GPR survey... show[s]... [hedge verb: "indicate,"
"suggest," "are consistent with"] the presence of [feature type]. [State what
independent context — e.g., known nearby excavated analogues, historical record —
supports the interpretation, if anything, while being explicit that this specific
anomaly is not yet confirmed].
```
*Writing instruction*: repeat this block for every distinct site/anomaly discussed. Vary confidence language deliberately per §10's five-tier hierarchy — never claim "chamber confirmed" language for an anomaly that has not been excavated. Always quantify at least one dimension (depth, size) per vignette. Pair every hard quantitative claim with a human-scale analogy where possible ("the size of a walk-in closet," "54 hectares").

### METHOD SPOTLIGHT (optional secondary section, e.g., "GPR Vector Imaging")
*Purpose*: when the paper showcases a specific processing/analytical innovation distinct from the general survey narrative, give it its own top-level header at the same hierarchical depth as the general Results section, and structure it as a self-contained mini Methods+Results.

```
When [condition triggering need for this method, e.g., significant topographic
relief] is present, [method name] is applied to [correct/enhance] the data by
[one-sentence mechanism description]. [State the one key assumption this method
relies on, explicitly, as a limitations statement]. [Name any side-effect artifact
the method introduces, and cross-reference the figure that illustrates it].

Applied to [specific case/target], the corrected dataset shows [key measured
feature, with dimension]. [State any negative/null results and their
archaeological interpretation — e.g., "no subsurface voids below X m, suggesting
only a shallow surface burial is present"]. [Close with a typological/chronological
interpretation connecting the geophysical result back to the archaeological
research question posed in the Introduction].
```
*Writing instruction*: this section's closing sentence should always loop back to answering an archaeological question (dating, typology, feature classification) — never let a method-spotlight section end on a purely technical note without archaeological payoff.

### DISCUSSION (implicit in Goodman 2014 — recommend making explicit for journal format)
*Writing instruction*: this paper has NO standalone Discussion section — its discussion content is distributed inline (limitation/assumption statements embedded in Method Spotlight; validation-hierarchy hedging embedded in each Results vignette). For a full journal manuscript, EXTRACT this distributed content into an explicit Discussion covering: (1) synthesis across all case vignettes — what pattern of feature types/depths emerges site-wide; (2) explicit limitations section (equipment: no real-time tilt monitoring; processing: crossover artifacts in vector-corrected volumes; interpretive: unconfirmed anomalies awaiting excavation); (3) comparison to prior work at the same or comparable sites (cite Edwards & Okita 2006 Tenri University keyhole-tomb GPR work as a comparator, following this paper's own reference list); (4) broader significance for heritage management (e.g., hazard — modern farm equipment falling into shallow chambers — as a practical/applied argument for continued survey).

### CONCLUSIONS (implicit — recommend making explicit for journal format)
*Writing instruction*: Goodman (2014) ends on a single case's chronological interpretation sentence rather than a synthesized Conclusions section. For journal format, add a short (3–6 sentence) Conclusions paragraph that: (1) restates the survey's overall success rate/coverage, (2) lists the confirmed vs. probable vs. unconfirmed feature counts across all sites discussed, (3) states the method's demonstrated reliability (e.g., excavation-confirmed detection rate), (4) proposes next steps (further excavation targets, extension to unsurveyed portions of the site).
