# Paper-Template Analysis #2 — Dalan, Bevan, Goodman et al. (2011), "The Measurement and Analysis of Depth in Archaeological Geophysics: Tests at the Biesterfeldt Site, USA"

> **IMPORTANT SOURCE-IDENTIFICATION NOTE (read first):** The file supplied was
> `# Dean Goodman - 2011 - The Measurement and Analysis of Depth in archaeological geophysics.pdf`.
> Having read the PDF in full, the actual paper is:
>
> **Dalan, R. A., Bevan, B. W., Goodman, D., Lynch, D., De Vore, S., Adamek, S., Martin, T., Holley, G. & Michlovic, M. (2011).** *The Measurement and Analysis of Depth in Archaeological Geophysics: Tests at the Biesterfeldt Site, USA.* Archaeological Prospection, 18(4), 245–265. DOI: 10.1002/arp.419.
>
> **Dean Goodman is the third of nine co-authors** (lead/corresponding author is **Rinita A. Dalan**), and the filename's attribution to "Dean Goodman" as sole/lead author is inaccurate — this is a multi-institution, multi-instrument collaborative field paper, not a Goodman-authored GPR-theory paper. It is filed in the researcher's library under Goodman's name presumably because GPR/GPR-SLICE is one of several methods discussed and Goodman is the GPR specialist co-author.
>
> **Consequence for this analysis:** the paper is **not** primarily an equation-heavy GPR velocity/depth-conversion paper as the task brief assumed. It is a **multi-technique comparative field-methods paper** (magnetometry, resistance, GPR time-slices/overlay analysis, resistivity pseudosections, and five distinct *downhole* probe techniques — susceptibility, magnetic viscosity, total field, capacitance, resistivity — plus a novel optical "direct-push colour" (DPC) sensor). Explicit displayed/numbered mathematical equations are **essentially absent from the running text** — the paper conveys quantitative method through calibration constants, instrument specifications, and cited external algorithms (e.g. Won & Bevis 1987; Blakely 1995) rather than through worked equations. This analysis documents the paper faithfully as it actually reads, and separately flags in Section 8 exactly what "equation-like" content does and does not exist, so the template built from it is not miscalibrated.
>
> Because this paper's structure differs substantially from a single-method GPR depth-theory paper, template designers should treat this file as the **"multi-technique depth-comparison field paper" archetype**, complementary to (not a duplicate of) a template built from a classic single-method GPR paper.

## 2. Section & Subsection Hierarchy

No decimal numbering is used anywhere in the paper (no "1.", "1.1" etc.) — this is a **Wiley Archaeological Prospection house style**: unnumbered bold/italic headings only, relying on typographic hierarchy (heading level = font weight/size), not numerals.

```
[Title block: Title / Authors / Affiliations / Abstract / Keywords]

Introduction                                            (H1, unlabelled)
Depth in archaeological geophysics                       (H1)
The Biesterfeldt Site                                     (H1)
Geophysical Area Surveys                                  (H1)
Methods for Geophysical Depth Investigations               (H1)
  Unploughed field: House 33 and ditch and House 20        (H2 — italic, no bold)
    Ground-penetrating radar overlay analyses               (H3 — bold, run-in style)
    Downhole susceptibility                                 (H3)
  Ploughed field: House E and the southern ditch            (H2)
    Downhole susceptibility                                 (H3)
    Downhole magnetic viscosity                             (H3)
    Downhole magnetic field, capacitance and resistivity     (H3)
    Resistivity pseudosection                                (H3)
    Direct-push colour                                       (H3)
Results of Geophysical Depth Investigations                (H1)
  Unploughed field: House 33 and ditch                       (H2)
  Unploughed field: House 20                                 (H2)
  Ploughed field: House E                                    (H2)
  Ploughed field: southern ditch                             (H2)
Discussion and conclusions                                  (H1 — merged D&C, no separate "Conclusions" head)
Acknowledgements                                            (H1)
References                                                  (H1, alphabetical, unnumbered)
```

**Key structural observations for template purposes:**

1. **Parallel H1/H2 architecture between Methods and Results.** "Methods for Geophysical Depth Investigations" and "Results of Geophysical Depth Investigations" use *identical* second-level subheadings (Unploughed field: House 33/House 20; Ploughed field: House E/southern ditch). This is a deliberate mirrored structure — the reader can flip between the Methods entry for a technique and its Results entry using the same subheading text. This "parallel scaffolding" is a strong, reusable device for any paper comparing multiple techniques across multiple sites/features.
2. **No separate "Study Area" + "Geophysical Survey" split** — instead, three back-to-back sections build context progressively: general depth theory → site background → prior area-survey results — before the paper ever gets to the paper's own new methods. This is a long, patient scene-setting run (roughly 40% of the paper) before original methodology begins.
3. **"Discussion and Conclusions" is fused into one section**, not split — common in shorter Wiley AP papers (~20 pages) where a standalone Conclusions section would be redundant.
4. **No standalone "Interpretation" section** — interpretation is threaded directly into Results paragraphs (each result is immediately glossed with an archaeological reading), rather than being deferred to a separate section. This is an important stylistic choice: Results and Interpretation are NOT separated in this paper (contrast with papers that keep raw findings and interpretive claims in different sections).

## 3. Section-by-Section Deep Analysis

### 3.1 Abstract

**Purpose & writing logic:** Functions as a compressed table of contents plus a headline result. Because the paper's core contribution is *breadth of technique comparison* rather than a single novel method, the abstract's job is to enumerate scope (so a reader scanning it knows immediately whether "their" technique is covered) and then reassure the reader that everything was validated by excavation.

**Paragraph organization:** Single paragraph — but internally has a clear 4-beat rhythm: (1) general framing statement, (2) methods enumeration, (3) site/management framing, (4) findings + validation + closing generalization. This 4-beat compression is typical of Wiley AP's abstract length limits (~250–300 words).

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Information on depth may be gained through the analysis of geophysical maps, by utilizing soundings, pseudosections, or time-slices, or via the application of downhole measurements."

Note the pattern: **abstract capability statement, framed passively ("Information... may be gained"), immediately followed by an enumerated list of the means of gaining it.** This is a "capability + taxonomy" opener — establishes the general problem space before naming the specific site.

**Typical content:** problem framing → site + technique inventory → what physical features were targeted → what was found → which techniques excelled at which sub-task → excavation validation → generalizable closing claim.

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "Various methods and techniques were compared at the Biesterfeldt site, a proto-historic earth-lodge village in the Northern Plains, USA."
- "Geophysical tests focused on depth yielded locations and depths for several lodges and the filled ditch as well as small and thin features and layers within them."
- "Downhole geophysical and direct-push color techniques were particularly helpful for mapping lodges in the plowed portion of the site. These results were confirmed through excavation."
- "These tests highlight the advantages of integrating techniques focused on depth with geophysical area surveys."

### 3.2 Introduction

**Purpose & writing logic:** The Introduction here does *not* immediately dive into the site. Instead it spends its first two paragraphs establishing the **institutional/administrative genesis** of the project (an NPS training course + a cooperative agreement), then the **conceptual goal** (augmenting 2D plan maps with depth), then explicitly previews the **paper's own organizational logic** in its final paragraph — a self-referential roadmap sentence, which is a strong, reusable device.

**Paragraph organization and flow:** (1) administrative/collaborative origin of fieldwork → (2) statement of the two-part goal (assess utility of depth techniques for archaeology; secondarily, develop/adapt instruments) → (3) contrast with typical area-survey goals (plan-view mapping) to justify novelty of a depth-focus → (4) practical framing: less destructive/costly than excavation, esp. valuable for "low-contrast earthen features" → (5) explicit roadmap paragraph telling the reader exactly how the rest of the paper is organized.

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Geophysical tests were conducted at the Biesterfeldt site (32RM1) in eastern North Dakota, USA in conjunction with a National Park Service (NPS) training course on archaeological prospection and with archaeological and geophysical investigations at the site by Minnesota State University Moorhead (MSUM)."

Pattern: **immediate site identification with official site number, location, and dual institutional trigger for the fieldwork** — no throat-clearing about "geophysics is important in archaeology" (that generic framing is entirely absent; this paper trusts its Archaeological Prospection readership to already know why depth matters, and gets straight to project provenance).

**Typical content:** why the fieldwork happened (funding/training-course origin), the specific dual goals, contrast with standard practice, and — critically — **a closing "roadmap" paragraph**:
> "The focus here is on the comparison of a wide range of geophysical depth investigations on similar features at a single site. The section on methods, which follows general discussions of depth in archaeological geophysics, the Biesterfeldt site, and geophysical area surveys at Biesterfeldt, is thus organized by the area of the site (unploughed versus ploughed) and the feature type (house, or earth lodge, and ditch) in which the various methods were applied."

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "The goal of the geophysical and direct-push colour (DPC) tests described here was to assess their utility for augmenting two-dimensional geophysical and other plan maps by adding detailed depth information that is critical for archaeological interpretation."
- "In contrast to the focus of most geophysical area surveys, i.e., making plan maps of the locations and shapes of archaeological features, these tests emphasized the measurement of geophysical and other properties as they changed with depth."
- "Such an approach has the potential for being particularly beneficial for the low-contrast earthen features that are characteristic of much of the North American prehistoric and proto-historic archaeological record."
- "Results are also presented by areas and feature type, with the final discussion and conclusion moving to implications of these results for general archaeological application."

### 3.3 "Depth in Archaeological Geophysics" (Background/Literature Section, functions as a mini-Review)

**Purpose & writing logic:** This is an unusual but very reusable section type: a **standalone conceptual/literature background section that is NOT called "Literature Review"** but does that work — surveying *why* depth matters archaeologically, *how* it can be measured (four procedures), and *what tradeoffs* each procedure carries — entirely before the site is properly introduced. It functions as the paper's theoretical scaffold, cited heavily, and it is explicitly structured as a **taxonomy of methods** (matching the abstract's taxonomy) that the rest of the paper will exemplify.

**Paragraph organization and flow:** (1) why depth information matters for archaeological interpretation (dating, volume, excavation budgeting, construction planning, cross-sections, formation processes) → (2) coring/borehole visual & instrumented inspection → (3) geophysical soundings (GPR, resistivity, EM, seismic) with citations → (4) technical depth-analysis of surface geophysical maps (magnetic/gravity anomaly broadening with depth) with citations → (5) a comparative pros/cons paragraph contrasting downhole vs at-surface approaches → (6) a second pros/cons paragraph specifically on at-surface analytical depth estimation limitations.

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Adding depth to locational information on archaeological features provides opportunity for the enhanced interpretation and management of archaeological features."

Pattern: **abstract capability/benefit statement stated as a general truth**, unattached to any citation — establishes the motivating premise before literature is invoked.

**Typical content:** conceptual justification for depth work; enumerated procedures (coring, downhole geophysics, soundings, surface-map depth-inversion); citation-dense paragraphs surveying prior archaeological applications of each; explicit tradeoff discussion (destructive/expensive vs non-destructive/less-accurate).

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "If the depth to both the top and the bottom of a feature may be determined, then the volume of that feature can be estimated."
- "These 'down-hole' measurements, however, circumvent the loss of resolution with depth that plagues 'at-surface' instruments and provide opportunity for the detection of thin strata and small features at any depth."
- "The estimation of depth by an analysis of at-surface readings is essentially non-destructive, and the surveys are fast in comparison with downhole surveys. However, the depths will always be less accurate than the measurements from bored holes and technical knowledge is required for an analysis of at-surface surveys."
- "Analysis of magnetic maps will yield depths to the middle of compact features, rather than to their upper surfaces."

This section is the closest thing in the paper to a formal **methodological rationale/theory section**, and its rhetorical move — stating a general claim, then immediately qualifying it with the opposite technique's tradeoff — is a reusable "compare-and-hedge" pattern for any Methods-justification paragraph.

### 3.4 Study/Site Section ("The Biesterfeldt Site")

**Purpose & writing logic:** Establishes archaeological/historical/cultural context (who lived there, when, what the site looked like, prior excavation history) *before* any new geophysics is described. Classic function: ground the reader in what is buried and why it matters culturally, so later geophysical results can be evaluated against known ethnographic/architectural expectations (e.g., "four central wood supports" primes the reader for the GPR search for four posts later in Results).

**Paragraph organization and flow:** (1) location + physical setting (bench overlooking a meander scar) + site size + prior excavation citation → (2) cultural-historical identity (Cheyenne, 18th century, map provenance, georeferencing) → (3) archaeological details of the ditch (dimensions from prior excavation) → (4) architectural details of lodges (size range, depth, construction technique, post arrangement, entryways, house typology — two types identified) → (5) the ploughed/unploughed dichotomy that structures the entire rest of the paper, explained historically (20th-century agricultural impact).

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "The Biesterfeldt village, located about 70 km southwest of Fargo, North Dakota, sits on an elevated bench overlooking an abandoned meander scar of the Sheyenne River (Figure 1)."

Pattern: **name + precise relative location (distance + direction from a known city) + landform description + immediate figure cross-reference.** This is the canonical archaeological site-description opener: always anchor a reader unfamiliar with the region using distance-from-known-place, then geomorphological setting, then a figure pointer in the very first sentence.

**Typical content:** location/geomorphology, culture-historical attribution, prior excavation/survey history, quantified feature dimensions from historic sources, architectural/construction detail relevant to what geophysics might later detect, and the taphonomic/land-use history (ploughing) that creates the paper's central methodological problem (why can't we just look — some houses are erased at the surface).

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "Biesterfeldt is a presumed early historic period village of the Cheyenne people, and its presence has been noted since 1799."
- "Based on excavations by Strong 1940, p. 371, the ditch was approximately 3 m wide and 1.5 m deep."
- "The lodges all had four central wood supports with a central hearth."
- "Starting at the beginning of the 20th century, the southern half of the site was repeatedly ploughed; this obliterated all at-surface traces of houses and the ditch in that area."

### 3.5 "Geophysical Area Surveys" (prior/context 2D survey section)

**Purpose & writing logic:** Bridges the site-history section and the new depth-focused methods. Establishes the plan-view baseline (magnetometry + resistance over the whole village) against which the new depth work will be compared and interpreted. This is functionally a "prior work at this site by us" section — distinct from general Literature Review, and distinct from the depth-specific new methods to follow.

**Paragraph organization and flow:** (1) instrument/survey-parameter statement (who collected what, with which instrument, what spacing) → (2) qualitative comparison of the resulting maps against the historic Libby-Stout map, feature by feature (houses, trail, fence line, hearths, entrances, ditch, plough scars) → concludes with a caveat about what is still ambiguous (low-contrast anomalies in the ploughed area).

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Magnetometer and resistance surveys of the entire Biesterfeldt village have been completed (Figures 1 and 2) in addition to smaller areas surveyed by other methods."

Pattern: **survey-completion statement + immediate dual figure reference**, followed in the very next sentences by full instrument specification (make/model, line spacing, sample density) — this is the template's canonical **"Methods sentence with embedded hardware spec"** style, reused throughout the paper (see 3.6).

**Typical content:** instrument make/model, acquisition geometry (line spacing, sample spacing, probe separation, orientation), then a systematic feature-by-feature comparison against the independently known ground truth (historic map), explicitly calling out what each method is/isn't good at ("Metal artefacts and central hearths were mapped by the magnetometer survey while Strong's excavations and pathways leading from house entrances were indicated by the resistance survey").

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "The magnetic data were collected during the week preceding the NPS workshop (De Vore, 2008) with a Bartington 601–2 dual fluxgate gradiometer using a 1-m line spacing and an eight-sample per m collection strategy."
- "A comparison of Figures 1 and 2 shows that all of the known houses and most of the pits in the unploughed (northern) portion of the site are identifiable in the geophysical data, although houses with either shallow or excavated (archaeologically) basins are not as distinct."
- "A series of 5–8 m wide swaths trending E–W in the southern half of the site are the result of ploughing; these anomalies make recognition of houses extremely difficult."
- "Despite this, these maps do indicate a number of low contrast anomalies that are at least suggestive of intact or partially intact house basins in the ploughed section of the site."

### 3.6 Methods ("Methods for Geophysical Depth Investigations")

**Purpose & writing logic:** This is the paper's methodological core, and it is organized **spatially/by-feature, not by-instrument** — i.e., the heading hierarchy is "Unploughed field → House 33/House 20" then "Ploughed field → House E/ditch," and *within* each spatial block, sub-subheadings name each technique applied there. This is a deliberate choice reflecting the paper's actual research logic (methods were deployed opportunistically per area, not uniformly across the whole site), and it is explicitly justified in the Introduction's roadmap paragraph.

Each technique sub-subsection follows a highly consistent internal template:
1. One-sentence rationale for the technique / why it was chosen for this location.
2. Full instrument specification: manufacturer, model number, sensor type, probe geometry/diameter.
3. Acquisition geometry: grid spacing, depth increments, starting depth, hole diameter/coring method.
4. Data-reduction/calibration detail: calibration constants, drift correction, software used, and — where applicable — cross-validation against an independent lab measurement.

**Opening sentence pattern (technique subsection):**
> "Ground-penetrating radar surveys, due to their high resolution and the depth information that they provide, enjoy wide application in archaeology."

> "Although Dalan and Goodman (2007) have previously used GPR time slice software to process downhole susceptibility data, the 2008 NPS course provided an opportunity to use this software to compare downhole susceptibility with GPR data over the same feature."

Pattern: each technique subsection opens with either (a) a **generic justificatory claim about the method's known strengths**, citing the broader literature, or (b) an explicit **continuity statement referencing the team's own prior work**, positioning the current test as an incremental extension. Both patterns avoid opening with raw acquisition parameters — the "why" always precedes the "how."

**Typical content per technique block:** instrument make/model (always specific — "Bartington Instruments MS2 meter and MS2H sensor"; "Gossen Geohm 3 meter"; "model C350 from GME"), physical probe construction detail (down to materials — "a cardboard tube with bands of aluminum foil around it"), spacing/interval parameters, calibration constants with explicit conversion factors, and software used for processing (GPR-SLICE v6.0 named repeatedly, Res2Dinv/Res2Dmod, ArchaeoSurveyor 3D).

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases (methods voice):**
- "The probe was simply a cardboard tube with bands of aluminum foil around it; it was insulated from the soil with a plastic sheet."
- "Multiplying values by 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ yields SI susceptibilities; this calibration factor is suggested by Bartington Instruments for 24-mm holes and was confirmed through comparison with samples collected from the cored holes and measured in the laboratory on a Bartington MS2B sensor."
- "The spatial resolution of the magnetic sensor is so high that a shift in its location by only 1 mm yields very different measurements."
- "Due to mechanical problems, poor data were obtained from the test at the west end of the transect (E947.5) and therefore the standard larger diameter truck-mounted system was used for subsequent tests..."

Note the last example — the paper is **candid about instrument failure and workaround decisions**, a notably honest, field-notebook-style voice rarely seen in more polished single-author papers. This transparency about mechanical/logistical hiccups is a genre marker of true multi-instrument field-comparison papers and is worth preserving in any template built from this archetype.

### 3.7 Results ("Results of Geophysical Depth Investigations")

**Purpose & writing logic:** Results are presented in the *same* spatial order as Methods (mirrored heading structure — see Section 2), and **interpretation is fused directly into the results narration**, not deferred. Each paragraph typically follows: describe the anomaly/pattern seen in a figure → immediately compare its dimensions/shape against the historic map or excavation record → offer an archaeological interpretation → note any discrepancy or uncertainty.

**Paragraph organization and flow (representative, House 33):** (1) describe the shallow time-slice pattern (ring shape, diameter) → (2) compare to Libby-Stout recorded dimensions → (3) note what is absent (no floor reflection) and hypothesize why → (4) describe the overlay-analysis result (four reflections in a rectangle) → (5) compare rectangle dimensions to excavation-documented post spacing → (6) note orientation matches known entrance direction → (7) give per-post depth ranges → (8) close with a consistency check.

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Shallow GPR time slices for the area over House 33 and the ditch are shown in Figure 3."

> "Figure 6 shows the top 16 (of the 46 total) 2-cm depth slices produced from the magnetic susceptibility data, representing depths between 10 and 40 cm."

Pattern: **every Results subsection opens with a direct figure pointer stating exactly what is shown and its parameters (slice count, depth range)** — description precedes interpretation by design; the reader is oriented to the figure before any claim is made about it.

**Typical content:** anomaly geometry/dimensions described in the same units and comparative frame as the site-history section (so numbers are directly checkable against Section 3.4's historic dimensions), archaeological interpretation offered immediately and often hedged ("may correspond to," "perhaps," "may locate"), explicit cross-method comparison within the same paragraph (GPR vs magnetic vs resistance for the same feature), and quantified uncertainty or negative results stated plainly ("None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit").

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "In the top slice, the house is clearly indicated by a ring-shaped reflection anomaly approximately 12 m in diameter, which agrees with the dimensions of House 33 recorded by Libby and Stout (Grinnell, 1918, pp. 362–363)."
- "Although the house is well defined by the radar at the surface, the bottom of the house, where we would expect a floor reflection, is not."
- "These reflections form a rectangle, approximately 3 × 2.6 m in size, that is near the upper end of the spacing (1.5–3 m) documented for central posts by Strong's excavations (Wood, 1971) but certainly in line with the relatively large diameter of this house."
- "The highest susceptibility values, interpreted as the central hearth, were observed at E957.3 where a burned soil was noted when coring and enhanced susceptibility values were documented between approximately 20 and 35 cm."
- "None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit."

### 3.8 Interpretation

**Note:** As stated in Section 2, this paper does **not** have a standalone Interpretation section — interpretive claims are embedded directly within Results (Section 3.7) and further synthesized in Discussion and Conclusions (Section 3.9). Template designers building a "separated Results/Interpretation" paper should NOT use this paper as the model for that split; use it instead as the model for a **fused Results-Interpretation style**, which is a legitimate and common alternative in field-methods papers where each anomaly needs immediate archaeological glossing to remain meaningful to the reader (raw geophysical values alone are uninformative without archaeological context attached in the same breath).

### 3.9 Discussion and Conclusions

**Purpose & writing logic:** Synthesizes across *all* techniques and *all* four site areas to answer the paper's real question — not "what did we find at Biesterfeldt" (answered in Results) but "**what does this tell us, generally, about how to do depth-focused archaeological geophysics**." This section explicitly returns to and answers the taxonomy set up in Section 3.3 (Depth in Archaeological Geophysics), closing the loop between literature-grounded theory and this paper's empirical test of it.

**Paragraph organization and flow:** (1) overall summary claim of value added → (2) at-surface + depth-focused integration benefit, with scope caveat (worked well for unploughed houses/ditch, less well for other ploughed features) → (3) GPR overlay analysis evaluation — success at House 33, explicit non-generalization caveat ("this technique... cannot be expected to produce this level of detail in every instance") using House 20 as the counter-example → (4) downhole-technique general capability claim (detecting thin strata invisible to any at-surface method) → (5) practical excavation-guidance payoff, described concretely (test-unit placement, confirmed hearth dimensions) → (6) ditch-specific synthesis across two independent magnetic and resistivity analyses → (7) DPC novel-instrument evaluation (first field trial) → (8) susceptibility-vs-magnetic-field methodological verdict (susceptibility generally better, with physical reasoning given) → (9) capacitance/resistivity evaluation, explicitly framed around lack of correlation being a *feature not a bug* (independence of information) → (10) forward-looking recommendations (rotational sampling around a borehole; finer depth intervals; standardizing hole diameters; adding downhole spectroscopy) → (11) explicit conditions-for-success caveat (Biesterfeldt was "a good site for borehole geophysics" partly by chance of soil type) → (12) closing generalization about combining depth techniques with area surveys.

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "The geophysical tests at the Biesterfeldt site have yielded valuable information for understanding the construction and use of the earth lodges (houses) and the encircling ditch as well as post-abandonment disturbance."

Pattern: **retrospective summary claim in present-perfect tense** ("have yielded"), immediately naming the three archaeological payoffs (construction/use, feature type, post-depositional process) — establishing the discussion's organizing categories before elaborating them.

**Typical content:** cross-technique synthesis, explicit statement of what generalizes vs what was site/case-specific, methodological verdicts (which instrument is "generally better" and *why*, with physical mechanism given), practical/field-workflow payoffs (excavation guidance, budget), forward-looking technical recommendations for future practitioners, and a closing return to the paper's opening framing (depth + area survey integration).

**3–5 verbatim characteristic sentences/phrases:**
- "This technique, however, cannot be expected to produce this level of detail in every instance; downhole susceptibility tests suggest that central posts were missed by the GPR survey over House 20."
- "The downhole tests demonstrated that there are thin strata or small features that can be detected only with downhole geophysics; these features will be invisible to geophysical measurements that are made at the surface."
- "While the lack of strong correlation may appear to make the data less reliable, it also indicates that the readings were independent and did not wastefully duplicate each other."
- "Biesterfeldt was a good site for borehole geophysics; this was partly because the locations for holes could be selected from both historical and at-surface geophysical maps... We were fortunate that the soil was not stoney, hard or water-saturated at Biesterfeldt; borehole geophysics could then have been impossible."
- "The tests at the Biesterfeldt site have demonstrated the benefits of combining geophysical depth information with at-surface surveys in order to define and explore features, to understand their stratigraphic context, and even to guide additional geophysical area surveys."

This section is notably **candid about limits and luck** ("We were fortunate...") — an honesty marker worth preserving; it distinguishes a rigorous field-comparison paper from a purely promotional methods paper.

### 3.10 Acknowledgements

**Purpose & writing logic:** Functions almost as a mini-provenance/credits appendix given the paper's large collaborative team — it is unusually long and specific for an Acknowledgements section (nearly a full paragraph plus), because so many of the paper's individual figures/datasets were produced by different named people/institutions (echoed later in explicit "Author:" credit blocks inside Figures 8 and 13 — an unusual and reusable device, see Section 6).

**Opening sentence pattern:**
> "Work at the Biesterfeldt site was stimulated by the purchase of the property by the Archaeological Conservancy in 2004 and a NPS initiative to nominate Biesterfeldt as a National Historic Landmark."

## 4. Writing Style

### Tense usage by section
| Section | Dominant tense | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | Simple past ("were compared," "provided") | Occasional present for general capability claims ("Information... may be gained") |
| Introduction | Simple past for what was done; present for general truths | "Geophysical tests were conducted..." vs "Detailed information... can enhance..." |
| Depth in Archaeological Geophysics (background) | Present tense throughout | Timeless/general-truth register: "Depths can be resolved...," "Analysis of magnetic maps will yield..." |
| Site section | Simple past (historical facts) + present (still-observable state) | "The village... sits on..." (present, still true) vs "the site was partially excavated..." (past) |
| Methods | Simple past, heavy passive voice | "Measurements were made...," "The probe was composed of..." |
| Results | Simple past for observations; present for figure-referencing | "Figure 3 shows..." (present) vs "the house is clearly indicated by..." (present, describing the persistent figure) — Results notably mixes past (what was measured) and present (what the figure/pattern shows) sentence by sentence |
| Discussion/Conclusions | Present perfect for cumulative claims; present for generalizations; conditional/modal for recommendations | "have yielded," "This... demonstrates...," "it might be beneficial to..." |
| Acknowledgements | Simple past | Uniformly past tense, factual/credit statements |

### Voice (active vs passive)
Overwhelmingly **passive voice in Methods** ("Measurements were made," "The probe was composed of," "Readings were taken at intervals of 2 cm") — standard for the instruments-and-procedures register, emphasizing the procedure over the actor. **Results and Discussion shift toward more active constructions when making interpretive claims** ("we would expect a floor reflection," "we may only be seeing the tops of these posts") — the collective "we" appears specifically at moments of interpretive judgment, marking a voice shift from procedural passive to authorial active.

### Sentence length/variety
Sentences are long and clause-dense throughout, frequently 30–50+ words with multiple subordinate clauses and parenthetical citations or figure references embedded mid-sentence. Short sentences are used deliberately for emphasis or to state a clean negative/limiting result, e.g. "None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit." and "Due to mechanical problems, viscosity data were not collected inside the ditch." This short-sentence-as-emphasis technique is a reusable device: bury procedural detail in long sentences, but isolate key findings/limitations in short, standalone ones.

### Common connectors and transition techniques
- **Contrast/hedging:** "although," "however," "while... ," "Despite this,"
- **Causal:** "because," "since," "due to," "As a result"
- **Additive/enumerative within paragraphs:** "In addition," "Furthermore," "Also,"
- **Comparative anchoring to prior data:** "As a comparison," "This agrees with," "which agrees with the dimensions... recorded by"
- **Cautious/hedged interpretation markers:** "may," "perhaps," "probably," "appears to," "is interpreted as," "suggests that" — a very high density of epistemic hedging language throughout Results and Discussion, appropriate to geophysical inference from indirect signals.
- **Section-bridging sentences:** the paper frequently closes one subsection with a sentence that previews the next area of investigation, e.g. ending the Introduction with an explicit roadmap, or ending "Geophysical Area Surveys" by flagging the ploughed-field ambiguity that "Methods for Geophysical Depth Investigations" then addresses directly.

## 5. Terminology & GPR/Geophysics Vocabulary

Because this is a multi-technique paper, vocabulary is introduced **method-by-method, each time the relevant technique is first used**, rather than in one consolidated glossary/terminology section. Pattern: **first mention of a technical term is immediately followed by either a plain-language gloss, a citation to the technique's originating literature, or both.**

| Term | How introduced (paraphrase of pattern) | Verbatim example |
|---|---|---|
| Time slices / amplitude slice mapping | Defined in-line at first use, with citation | "Using time slices, or amplitude slice mapping, reflections can be separated into horizontal slices, each corresponding to a distinct depth in the ground, upon which the intensity of reflections at that depth are mapped (Goodman et al., 1995)." |
| Overlay analysis | Named as "a special process called X," with citation, then explained procedurally | "a special process called overlay analysis (Goodman et al., 2009) was used to further image important reflections. In this process, the strongest reflectors across a range of time slices are placed onto a single two-dimensional image." |
| Microwave velocity / depth calibration | Introduced via the specific field procedure used (hyperbola fitting), not via general theory | "Depth calibration for the site was estimated by matching to a faint hyperbola seen on one of the radargrams. An estimated microwave velocity of 7 cm ns⁻¹ was measured." — Note: this is the **only explicit velocity/depth-conversion statement in the entire paper**, and it is a single sentence with no derivation, no equation, and no discussion of hyperbola-fitting mathematics. |
| Magnetic susceptibility / SI susceptibility | Introduced via instrument + calibration constant, not via physical-property first-principles definition | "Multiplying values by 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ yields SI susceptibilities; this calibration factor is suggested by Bartington Instruments for 24-mm holes..." |
| Magnetic viscosity / frequency dependence of susceptibility (κfd, χfd) | Introduced via a dedicated explanatory paragraph citing the underlying physics literature, distinguishing real vs imaginary susceptibility components and three physical mechanisms | "Low-field, alternating current (a.c.) susceptibility measurements (frequency domain) can be divided into real (in-phase) and imaginary (quadrature, or out-of-phase) components (Thompson and Oldfield, 1986). Three main mechanisms can give rise to the imaginary component: (i) eddy currents, (ii) weak-field hystersis and (iii) magnetic viscosity (Jackson, 2003–4)." |
| Electrical capacitance / dielectric constant (relative permittivity) | Introduced with citation list and immediate practical link to moisture content | "Electrical capacitance is related to the dielectric constant (also called relative permittivity) of the soil. The moisture content of soil has a major effect on its dielectric constant (Wolfe, 1962; Thomas, 1966; Berry et al., 1968; Wobschall, 1978; Okamoto, 1980; Curtis, 2001)." |
| Resistivity pseudosection | Defined functionally by what it is used for, before any acquisition detail | "An electrical pseudosection allows an approximation of electrical properties across a profile or cross-section. This pseudosection can be used to suggest both the location of a geophysical anomaly, and also the depth of the feature that caused it." |
| Pole–pole array/configuration | Named without definition — assumed reader familiarity (electrode array geometry is treated as standard-vocabulary, unlike the more novel terms above) | "Resistivity measurements were taken using the pole–pole configuration of electrodes." |
| Direct-push colour (DPC) | Most heavily explained term in the paper — full standalone subsection devoted to instrument physics, spectral range, and output data types, since it is the paper's most novel technique | "White light illuminates the soil and the reflection of that light is analysed. For each data point, the instrument records an entire reflection spectrum from 350 nm to 1000 nm and converts that spectrum into RGB and Munsell colour values." |

**Pattern to extract for template use:** term-introduction rigor is proportional to term novelty. Standard/well-known geophysics vocabulary (pole-pole array, gradiometer, dielectric constant) gets a one-line functional gloss or none at all. Novel/first-field-trial terminology (overlay analysis, DPC, MVM1 magnetic viscosity meter) gets a full paragraph of mechanism explanation plus citation to the originating source.

## 6. Figure Integration

**Referencing style:** Figures are cited with "(Figure N)" parenthetically inline, very frequently — almost every substantive claim in Results carries a figure pointer. Multiple figures are sometimes cross-referenced against each other within the same sentence ("A comparison of Figures 1 and 2 shows...").

**Figure types used:** site maps (feature outlines over survey extent, Figure 1); paired greyscale geophysical maps (magnetometry + resistance, Figure 2); grid arrays of time-slice sub-panels plus a derived overlay-analysis composite (Figures 3, 5, 6); composite comparison figures juxtaposing multiple survey types against a historic map (Figure 4); downhole log/profile plots at multiple boreholes overlaid (Figure 7); **dashboard-style multi-track downhole comparison figures** combining 5+ instrument tracks plus a photograph plus a stratigraphic color column plus an explicit "who collected what" credit table beneath the plot (Figures 8, 13 — an unusual, highly reusable device for multi-instrument papers); schematic interpreted cross-sections (Figure 9); forward-model comparison plots (measured vs calculated curves, Figure 10); resistivity pseudosection contour plots (Figure 11); dipole-model magnetic anomaly maps (measured vs calculated gradient, Figure 12).

**Caption style — verbatim examples:**

> "Figure 3. Shallow time slices for House 33 and the ditch area at the Biesterfeldt site. The top time slice was overweighted and the deeper reflections were underweighted such that only the strongest reflections would appear on the overlay map, shown below. Each slice has been independently colourized. Relatively strong reflections are shown by warm colours (red, orange) whereas weak reflections correlate with cool colours (blue, green). In the interpreted image the extent of the house as recorded by Libby and Stout (solid line) as well as the visible depression (dashed line) are shown, together with four possible posthole reflections, a pit and the ditch."

> "Figure 8. Comparison of downhole geophysical and direct-push colour data at House E. The near-infrared (IR) and red and blue data are presented as fractions of the total RGB spectrum. As the fraction of green light was rather uniform, it is not included. For red and blue reflectivity, the trace on the left side of the plot maps the fraction of blue reflection, the trace on the right side the fraction of red reflection, while the shaded area in between highlights changing differences between those colours."

> "Figure 12. An analysis of six magnetic anomalies in the southern ditch area. These anomalies were modelled as dipoles (×) or clusters of dipoles; the variable Z indicates their elevation (depth in m below surface) and the parameter M indicates their total magnetic moment (in Am²). Anomaly #4 corresponds to a section of the southern ditch. Anomaly #1 is also indicated on the cross-section in Figure 9."

**Caption structure pattern (reusable):** (1) one-sentence identification of what/where, (2) processing/production method explanation (how the image was made — overweighting, colourization, model parameters), (3) legend/symbol-key explanation (color meaning, symbol meaning), (4) cross-reference to other figures showing the same feature from another data type. Captions function as **mini-methods paragraphs**, not just labels — a reader could largely reconstruct the processing pipeline from captions alone.

## 8. Equation/Mathematical Presentation — CRITICAL CORRECTION TO TASK BRIEF

**The task brief describes this paper as "EQUATION-HEAVY (depth/velocity math)" with numbered, worked equations for hyperbola fitting, migration, two-way travel time, dielectric permittivity, and depth conversion. Having read the full paper cover to cover, this description does NOT match the actual paper.** There are **zero displayed, numbered equations anywhere in the text** (no "Eq. (1)", no LaTeX-style math blocks, no boxed formulas). All quantitative/mathematical content is conveyed in one of three ways:

1. **In-line calibration constants stated as plain-language multiplication factors**, e.g.:
   > "Multiplying values by 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ yields SI susceptibilities..."

2. **A single stated velocity value with no derivation shown**, e.g.:
   > "Depth calibration for the site was estimated by matching to a faint hyperbola seen on one of the radargrams. An estimated microwave velocity of 7 cm ns⁻¹ was measured."
   (Hyperbola-fitting for velocity is *referenced as a procedure* — "matching to a faint hyperbola" — but the geometry/mathematics of hyperbola fitting, migration, or two-way-travel-time-to-depth conversion is **never spelled out**, not even in prose form. The reader is assumed to already know how hyperbola-velocity-fitting works.)

3. **Citations to external algorithmic sources for the modeling actually performed**, with parameters reported as figure-embedded values (Z = depth, M = magnetic moment) rather than as equations in the text:
   > "The magnetic data within a 20-m square in this same area were further analysed using a computer program which assumes that features can be approximated as clusters of dipoles (Blakely, 1995)."
   > "This comparison was carried out with... two computer programs Res2Dinv and Res2Dmod from M. H. Loke (www.geoelectrical.com)."
   > "...this allows the data to be analysed with a two-dimensional magnetic model (Won and Bevis, 1987)."

   In each case the mathematical machinery (polygon/dipole gravitational-magnetic anomaly formulas, resistivity forward/inverse modeling) is **entirely outsourced to a cited external program/paper**; the host paper reports only inputs (electrode spacing, model geometry) and outputs (Z, M, depth-to-fill estimates) as figure annotations, never as derivations.

**What IS equation-like in this paper (the closest analogues):**
- Model parameters displayed as labeled figure annotations rather than as equations: Figure 9's stratigraphic cross-section labels each layer with `k = [susceptibility value] ppt` and `p = [resistivity value] ohm-m` — i.e., physical-property values are tabulated graphically on the figure rather than presented as a table or equation.
- Figure 12's dipole-model annotations: "Z = -0.8, M = 0.02" etc. — again, model *outputs*, not the underlying formula.
- The frequency-dependence-of-susceptibility discussion (Section 3.5/Methods, downhole magnetic viscosity) is the most conceptually mathematical passage in the paper, distinguishing real vs. imaginary susceptibility components — but this too is prose-only, citing Thompson & Oldfield (1986) for the underlying formalism rather than reproducing it.

**Implication for template building:** If the user's goal is a template for an **equation-heavy GPR depth/velocity paper** (hyperbola fitting, migration, two-way-travel-time, dielectric-permittivity depth conversion, worked and numbered), **this Dalan et al. 2011 paper is the wrong source text** — it should NOT be used as the equation-presentation model. It should instead be filed as the template for: **"how a multi-technique comparison paper handles quantitative rigor without displayed equations"** — i.e., via named calibration constants, external algorithm citations, and figure-embedded model parameters. If a genuinely equation-heavy Goodman GPR paper is needed (e.g., covering the standard $ v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r} $ velocity-permittivity relation, $ d = vt/2 $ two-way-travel-time depth conversion, or hyperbola-asymptote velocity fitting), a different Goodman-authored or Conyers-authored methodological paper should be sourced and analyzed separately — the 1993/1994/1995/2006/2014 Goodman papers or the Conyers & Goodman 1997 book in the same folder are more likely candidates and should be checked before building the equation-heavy template component.

## 9. Citation Style

**In-text format:** Author-date, e.g. "(Goodman et al., 1995)", "(Wood, 1971, p. 46)", integrated both parenthetically and as sentence subjects ("Wood (1971, p. 9) states that...").

**Density:** Very high in the background/literature section (3.3) — often 4–6 citations bundled in a single sentence as a "see also" list, e.g. "Depth analysis of magnetic data from archaeological sites has been done by Ralph (1965), Dittrich and Koppelt (1997), Neubauer and Eder-Hinterleitner (1997), Eppelbaum et al. (2001), Cucarzi et al. (2001) and Abbas et al. (2005)." Density drops substantially in Results, where citations are used almost exclusively to validate a measured dimension against a historic/prior-excavation source rather than to invoke methodological literature.

**Placement:** Almost always sentence-final or clause-final (rarely opens a sentence with the citation itself); page numbers given for historical-source claims ("Wood 1971, pp. 11–12") but typically omitted for general geophysics-method citations.

**Framing of prior work:** Three distinct framings recur:
1. **Foundational-citation framing** — citing a textbook/monograph as the authority behind a stated general principle (Blakely 1995 for potential-field theory, Thompson & Oldfield 1986 for magnetic viscosity mechanisms).
2. **Self-citation/continuity framing** — citing the authors' own prior Biesterfeldt work (Dalan et al., 2007; Dalan and Goodman, 2007; Dalan, 2009) to establish that the current paper extends an existing, ongoing research program rather than starting from scratch.
3. **Cross-validation framing** — citing an independent historic source (Grinnell 1918 reporting Libby and Stout's 1908 measurements; Wood 1971's excavation report) specifically to check a new geophysical measurement against ground truth, e.g. "which agrees with the dimensions of House 33 recorded by Libby and Stout (Grinnell, 1918, pp. 362–363)."

## 10. Interpretation/Analysis Methodology — How Rigor Is Presented

Because there is no separate "Interpretation" section, rigor in interpretation is demonstrated through five recurring rhetorical/analytical moves, used consistently across Results and Discussion:

1. **Dimension cross-checking.** Every new geophysical measurement of a feature's size/depth is immediately checked against an independent source (historic map, prior excavation report, or a different geophysical method run over the same feature), and agreement/disagreement is stated explicitly with a magnitude, never just "consistent" or "inconsistent" vaguely.
2. **Explicit negative results.** The paper states plainly when a method failed to detect something expected ("None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit"; central posts "missed by the GPR survey over House 20"), rather than omitting negative findings — a marker of methodological honesty valuable in a comparison paper whose entire purpose is to help future practitioners choose the right tool.
3. **Mechanistic explanation for correlations/non-correlations.** When two techniques agree or disagree, the paper offers a physical reason why, not just the observation — e.g. explaining that susceptibility and viscosity correlate because both respond to fine magnetic grain concentration, and explicitly noting that in different mineralogical settings this correlation would not be expected to hold.
4. **Explicit scope-limiting caveats after a strong positive result.** Immediately after reporting the GPR overlay's "remarkable" success at House 33, the very next sentences state the limit: "This technique, however, cannot be expected to produce this level of detail in every instance" — the paper never lets a single success stand as a general claim without also citing the counter-example (House 20) in the same breath.
5. **Attribution of favorable outcomes partly to site conditions/luck, not just technique quality.** "Biesterfeldt was a good site for borehole geophysics; this was partly because... We were fortunate that the soil was not stoney, hard or water-saturated" — explicitly separating "the technique worked" from "the technique will always work," a rigor marker that keeps the paper's conclusions properly bounded.
