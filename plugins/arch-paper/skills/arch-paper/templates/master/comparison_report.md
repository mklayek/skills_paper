# Comparison Report: Three Archaeological-GPR Papers as a Reusable Writing Template

This report synthesizes the per-paper analyses of three archaeological ground-penetrating-radar (GPR) papers into a single reusable backbone for a master template. The three sources are:

- **Paper 1** — Goodman, Nishimura and Rogers (1995), "GPR Time Slices in Archaeological Prospection." A short (5 page), figure-heavy methods paper in *Archaeological Prospection*, built around three parallel case studies (Nutubaru, Matsuzaki, Spiro).
- **Paper 2** — Dalan, Bevan, Goodman et al. (2011), "The Measurement and Analysis of Depth in Archaeological Geophysics: Tests at the Biesterfeldt Site, USA." A longer (21 page) multi-technique field-comparison paper in *Archaeological Prospection*, with mirrored Methods and Results, a fused Discussion and Conclusions, and no equations.
- **Paper 3** — Goodman, Higashi, Hongo and Nishimura (2014), "The Search for Subterranean Kofun Burial Chambers on the Island of Kyushu, Japan using Ground Penetrating Radar." A short (9 page) figure-driven case-study chapter with a five-tier evidentiary-validation hierarchy and no abstract.

## 2. Common Patterns (the Reusable Backbone)

These are the devices shared by all three papers. They form the core of the master template regardless of length or venue.

- **No decimal section numbering.** All three use bare, named, typographically ranked headings. Numbering is either supplied by the parent volume or simply absent. The backbone should default to named, unnumbered sections.
- **Author-date or bracketed references, alphabetical, unnumbered list, with self-citation for method continuity.** Every paper cites the authors' own prior work to establish a method lineage rather than re-deriving it, and every paper treats grey literature (conference proceedings, internal reports) as first-class citable sources.
- **Passive voice dominates the methods, with first person avoided or reserved for judgment.** Paper 1 and Paper 3 never use "we." Paper 2 uses "we" only at explicit interpretive moments. The default register for procedure description is passive.
- **Historical and archaeological context comes before the technique.** In every paper the reader is grounded in what is buried, when it was built, and why it matters, before any radar data is interpreted. Vocabulary (moat, chamber, keyhole tomb, earth lodge) is defined by use-in-context up front so later results sections do not need to re-explain it.
- **Interpretation is hedged and evidence-layered, scaled to available corroboration.** All three papers grade their confidence language. A claim gets a flat factual statement only when independently ground-truthed (excavation, historic map, aerial photo); otherwise it takes hedges like "is believed to be," "indicate," or "suggests." This graded honesty is the single most consistent interpretive signature across the set.
- **Ground-truth cross-checking is the primary validation currency.** Every paper checks a geophysical measurement against an independent source: excavation artifacts (Papers 1 and 3), historic map dimensions (Papers 1 and 2), a second data view of the same feature (Paper 1), or a different instrument over the same feature (Paper 2). Statistical anomaly-confidence measures are never used.
- **Figures carry interpretive weight; captions are not bare labels.** In all three, captions state what the figure means, not just what it shows, and often carry quantified results. A reader skimming only figures and captions could reconstruct most findings.
- **No tables and no equations.** None of the three papers uses a formal numbered table or a displayed equation. Quantitative content is delivered as in-text values, calibration constants stated in prose, and figure-embedded model parameters, with the underlying mathematics deferred to cited references.
- **Results and interpretation are fused, not separated.** No paper defers interpretation to a standalone section. Each anomaly is glossed archaeologically in the same breath it is described, because raw geophysical values are uninformative without immediate archaeological context.
- **The paper closes by returning to the archaeological question.** Every paper ends on archaeological or method-utility significance (a chronological inference, a heritage-management payoff, a defense of the method's utility) rather than on a bare geophysical observation. GPR is framed as instrumental to an archaeological end, never as an end in itself.

## 3. Divergences and What Drives Them

The three papers diverge mainly along the axis of **length and venue** and along the axis of **single-method case study versus multi-technique comparison**.

- **Abstract presence and style is driven by venue.** The journal short paper (Paper 1) has a compact scope-promise abstract. The full journal article (Paper 2) has a long, structured method-inventory abstract with a validation clause. The edited-volume chapter (Paper 3) has no abstract at all, letting the Introduction stand in. Venue conventions, not authorial preference, drive this.
- **Section depth is driven by length.** Paper 3 has one heading level and three body headers. Paper 1 is flat with Methods buried in the Introduction. Paper 2, being four times longer, sustains a three-level hierarchy and spends roughly 40% of its length on patient scene-setting (depth theory, then site, then prior area surveys) before its own methods begin.
- **Methods and Results organization is driven by method count.** The single-method case papers (1 and 3) organize by site or case vignette. The multi-technique paper (2) organizes by site area and feature type, then mirrors that exact subheading structure between Methods and Results so a reader can flip between a technique's method entry and its result entry. Mirrored scaffolding is a response to comparing many techniques, not a universal requirement.
- **Citation density is driven by both length and purpose.** Paper 2's comparison purpose and length support ~45 references and a citation-dense theory section. The compressed case studies (1 and 3) cite sparingly and only at the exact point a claim needs backing, using citation-as-definition for jargon.
- **Discussion and Conclusions handling is driven by length.** Paper 1 needs only a short standalone Conclusions. Paper 2 fuses Discussion and Conclusions into one section, standard for a ~20 page paper where a separate Conclusions would be redundant. Paper 3 has neither header and distributes the content inline, appropriate to a compressed chapter.
- **Register is driven by audience.** Paper 3, aimed at a general or edited-volume archaeology readership, adopts an evocative "search for" narrative voice, animates the landscape as an actor, and pairs every hard number with a human-scale analogy ("as large as a walk-in closet"). The journal papers (1 and 2) use a more neutral technical register, though Paper 2 remains candid and field-notebook-honest about instrument failures.
- **Candor about limits scales with the comparison stakes.** Paper 2, whose entire purpose is helping practitioners choose a tool, is the most explicit about negative results, instrument failures, and lucky site conditions. The single-method papers hedge interpretation but report fewer outright failures.

## 4. Best Practices to Adopt in the Master Template

Ranked from strongest and most broadly reusable to more situational.

1. **Graded evidentiary-validation honesty (Paper 3's five-tier hierarchy).** Explicitly rank every interpretation from excavation-confirmed ground truth, through morphological or typological reasoning, down to unconfirmed prospective anomaly, and match the hedging language to the tier. This is the single most valuable device in the set and generalizes to any inference-from-indirect-signal discipline.
2. **The repeatable anomaly-interpretation chain (Paper 1's 8-step formula).** Parameters, observe, quantify, hedged label, nested sub-feature, cross-view validation, mechanistic citation, confound caveat. A disciplined skeleton that keeps every interpretation transparent and auditable.
3. **Ground-truth cross-checking with stated magnitudes (all three, strongest in Paper 2).** Never say "consistent" vaguely; state the measured dimension and the independent benchmark and quantify the agreement or disagreement.
4. **Negative-results honesty (Paper 2).** State plainly when a method failed to detect an expected feature ("None of the methods appear to detect the northern pit"). In a methods or comparison paper, negative results are as valuable as positive ones.
5. **Self-sufficient, interpretive figure captions (all three, strongest in Paper 3).** Write captions that carry the finding and at least one quantified value, so figures plus captions reconstruct the paper. Include the archetypal figure set: regional context map, composite validation figure (radar plus 3D render plus excavation photo), GIS-draped depth slice, method-demonstration figure, and multi-panel depth-slice progression.
6. **Historical-context-before-technique framing (all three).** Open with cultural and historical stakes, define recurring feature vocabulary, then introduce the method. This forward-loads the vocabulary that Results will lean on.
7. **Parallel structure for multiple cases or techniques (Papers 1 and 2).** Repeat an identical case-study block per site (Paper 1), or mirror Methods and Results subheadings exactly when comparing techniques (Paper 2). Parallel scaffolding makes a multi-case paper navigable.
8. **Candor about scope limits and luck (Paper 2).** After a strong positive result, immediately state the counter-example or the condition under which the method would fail ("We were fortunate that the soil was not stoney"). Never let a single success stand as a general claim.
9. **Explicit statement of what was NOT done, with physical justification (Paper 1).** Naming an omitted processing step and justifying it (or flagging an unsolved open problem by name) is a legitimate, citable contribution in this genre.
10. **Mechanistic explanation for agreements and disagreements (Paper 2).** When two methods agree or a pattern appears, give the physical reason, not just the observation.
11. **Figure-level credit blocks for multi-author data streams (Paper 2).** For collaborative multi-instrument papers, credit each data stream on the figure itself, an elegant alternative to a contributions table.
12. **Human-scale analogies paired with hard numbers (Paper 3).** State the number, then anchor it to something a general reader can picture. Effective for broad-audience or heritage-facing venues.

## 5. Recommended Canonical Section Order

A full journal-style archaeological GPR paper synthesized from all three, with short-format collapses noted. Sections marked optional can be omitted for compressed chapters.

1. **Title** — technique plus key output plus site or region. Use an evocative "Search for / Discovery of" verb for broad-audience venues, a neutral noun phrase for strict geophysics journals. A colon-split conceptual clause plus concrete site clause (Paper 2 style) works well for method-comparison papers.
2. **Authors and affiliations** — pair the geophysical-method specialist (lead, corresponding) with local site-authority co-authors and a national or regional institute archaeologist.
3. **Abstract** — mandatory for journals, ~150 to 300 words. Cover context, the archaeological gap, method and key parameters, headline results with at least one quantified finding, and a validation statement. Omit only for edited-volume chapters.
4. **Keywords** — 3 to 6 terms, ordered general to novel: culture/period plus feature type, primary method, method-category umbrella term.
5. **Introduction** — historical and cultural context, narrowing to the site, the loss or knowledge-gap that justifies the survey, definition of recurring feature vocabulary, and an explicit roadmap paragraph stating how the paper is organized.
6. **Conceptual / theory background** (optional, for longer papers) — a standalone method-taxonomy section, not labeled "Literature Review," surveying why the problem matters and the tradeoffs of each candidate approach, foreshadowing the paper's own choice.
7. **Site / geological setting** — precise relative location plus landform plus figure pointer in the first sentence; cultural-historical identity and dating; quantified feature dimensions from historic sources; construction detail relevant to what GPR can detect; the taphonomic or land-use history that creates the methodological problem. May be placed retrospectively after the first result in compressed formats.
8. **Prior / baseline area surveys** (optional) — instrument and acquisition parameters, then a feature-by-feature comparison against independent ground truth, closing on what remains ambiguous.
9. **Materials and methods / data acquisition and processing** — instrument make, model, and geometry; ground conditions and penetration; the processing chain; depth-slice or volume generation. State explicitly what was NOT done and why. Compress to one dense paragraph for short formats; split and justify each step, and consider a survey-parameters table and a depth-slice-amplitude equation, for full journal format.
10. **Results** (repeat one block per site, anomaly, or technique; mirror the Methods subheading order) — open with a direct figure pointer stating what is shown and its parameters; describe geometry and dimensions in the same units as the site section; cross-check against the historic or excavation record with a stated magnitude; interpret immediately with tier-appropriate hedging; report negative results plainly; note orientation and alignment checks.
11. **Method spotlight** (optional) — a self-contained mini Methods-plus-Results for a specific processing innovation, at the same heading level as Results, closing on an archaeological payoff.
12. **Discussion** (fuse with Conclusions for shorter papers) — cross-technique or cross-site synthesis; verdict per technique with an explicit scope-limiting caveat citing the counter-example; mechanistic explanation for agreements or disagreements; a candor paragraph attributing success partly to site conditions; practical or heritage-management payoff; comparison to prior work at comparable sites.
13. **Conclusions** — restate the core contribution generalized beyond the specific sites; where useful, rebut a real methodological objection using evidence already presented (Paper 1's "defend and extend"); list confirmed versus probable versus unconfirmed feature counts; propose next steps; close on broader significance for the discipline.
14. **Acknowledgements** — funding and permit or access credit; named local and logistical contacts by name and role; workshop or training context; an explicit software or tool attribution sentence.
15. **References** — author-date or bracketed, alphabetical, unnumbered list. Include self-citation for method continuity, at least one classic geophysics textbook for any named-but-unelaborated technique, and grey literature on equal footing.
16. **Figures and captions** — inline near first citation for modern journals, or batched at the end for camera-ready proceedings. Captions self-sufficient and interpretive.

## 6. Pitfalls to Avoid (Venue-Specific Devices, Do Not Copy Blindly)

- **Do not skip the abstract.** Paper 3 omits it because edited-volume chapters allow that. Any journal submission requires a structured abstract with a quantified result and a validation clause. Treat the abstract as mandatory unless the specific venue explicitly waives it.
- **Do not bury Methods inside the Introduction.** Paper 1's italic-sub-heading Methods is a 1990s short-paper convention. A modern journal expects a clearly headed Methods or Data Acquisition and Processing section.
- **Do not leave Discussion and Conclusions content only distributed inline.** Paper 3 distributes it across Results and the method spotlight. For a full manuscript, extract it into explicit Discussion and Conclusions sections covering synthesis, limitations, prior-work comparison, and next steps.
- **Do not assume equation-free is always acceptable.** All three papers avoid displayed equations, deferring mathematics to citations. This is fine for case studies and comparison papers, but a methods-contribution paper aimed at a geophysics audience should present at least the depth-slice amplitude computation and the velocity-depth relation explicitly. The absence of equations is a genre trait of these three, not a universal standard.
- **Do not carry over internal inconsistencies and roughness.** Paper 1 spells "ground-penetrating radar" two ways and misspells an author name; Paper 3 contains a minor grammatical slip. These are transcription-faithful observations, not models to emulate. Enforce consistent terminology and clean grammar.
- **Do not over-narrate for a strict geophysics journal.** Paper 3's evocative "search for" register, animated landscape subjects, and human-interest asides suit an edited volume or general-archaeology audience. A technical geophysics journal expects a more neutral register; calibrate the narrative warmth to the venue.
- **Do not replicate very low citation density in a research article.** Paper 3's five references and citation-as-definition strategy work for a compressed chapter whose readership includes specialists. A standalone journal article needs fuller in-line glossing of jargon and a broader, denser reference base (roughly 15 to 40).
- **Do not default to figures-at-end placement.** Paper 3 batches figures after the References because that is a camera-ready proceedings convention. Most modern journals want figures inline near first citation. Decide placement by venue, since it changes the in-text referencing rhythm.
- **Do not adopt the software-only Acknowledgements.** Paper 3's single-sentence software credit, with no funding, permit, or crew thanks, is a compressed-format artifact (and doubles as a promotion of the lead author's own commercial product). A full paper should acknowledge funding, permissions, and field crews as well.
- **Do not treat the figure-level credit block as a substitute for methods rigor.** Paper 2's dashboard credit blocks are elegant for multi-author data streams, but they replace a contributions table, not the methods description. Keep full instrument and processing detail in the text.

---

**Strongest common patterns (summary):**
1. All three ground the reader in historical and archaeological context before the technique, define feature vocabulary up front, and close by returning to the archaeological question, treating GPR as instrumental rather than as an end in itself.
2. All three grade interpretive confidence honestly to available corroboration, using hedges like "is believed to be" or "indicate" for unconfirmed anomalies and flat statements only for excavation- or map-verified claims, with ground-truth cross-checking as the sole validation currency.
3. All three fuse Results and interpretation, avoid tables and equations, and lean on self-sufficient, interpretive figure captions that carry quantified findings.
4. All three use unnumbered named sections, passive-voiced methods with first person avoided or reserved for judgment, and author-date or bracketed references with self-citation for method continuity and grey literature on equal footing.
